# Part 41 — Multi-Role Analysis

## 41.0 How this chapter works, and what it is for

Parts 0 through 31 are a specification written by people who agreed with each other. This part is the first place in the document set where the specification is read by people whose professional incentives conflict, and where the reading is adversarial by design. Nine roles are given the same forty-one-chapter corpus and asked the same four questions: what is right here and why; what is wrong, missing, under-specified or over-specified; what would you need answered before you signed; and what would you add that nobody else in this room would think to require.

A multi-role review that produces nine approvals is a review that was not performed. The value of this chapter is entirely in its findings, and the findings below are deliberately concrete: each one names the chapter, the section, the feature ID, the table or the column it concerns, states the consequence in terms of a thing that will go wrong, and — where two chapters disagree — names both sides so that the reconciliation in Part 42 has something to reconcile rather than a sentiment to adjudicate.

**Severity scale**, used identically by all nine roles:

| Severity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **S1 — Blocking** | The build cannot start, or cannot start on this part of the product, until this is resolved. An implementer reaching this point must stop and ask, or will produce something demonstrably wrong. |
| **S2 — Major** | The build can start, but this will cause rework, a launch-blocking defect, a missed commitment or an unmanaged risk if it is not resolved before the module it touches is built. |
| **S3 — Minor** | A defect of precision, consistency or completeness that costs time and credibility but not correctness. Fix at the next editorial pass. |

**Verdict vocabulary.** *Approve* means the role would sign Part 18 §18.16 as it stands. *Approve with conditions* means the role would sign only if the named conditions are recorded as commitments with owners. *Reject* means the role would not sign, and names what would change that.

A note on tone. Every role below is written as that professional would actually write — the Business Analyst counts things, the Backend Architect worries about lock ordering at three in the morning, the CEO does not care about the invalidation map. Where a role is wrong about something another role knows better, that disagreement is preserved rather than smoothed, because it is the raw material of Part 42's conflict register.

## 41.1 The Business Analyst

### 41.1.1 What is right

This is the most completely specified small-business product I have reviewed, and the completeness is structural rather than decorative. Four things deserve to be said before the findings, because they are unusual enough that losing them in a rewrite would be a real cost.

**The identifier scheme actually works.** Canon §0.5 fixes `<MODULE>-<NN>` and every downstream artefact honours it: Part 16 catalogues by ID, Part 12 §12.2 enumerates the MVP by ID, Part 13 sequences by ID, Part 14 §14.1–14.8 lists each persona's features by ID, Part 15 annotates every journey step with the ID that governs it, and Part 17's FRDs are keyed by it. I can start from "Suresh needs `LED-06`" and arrive at the reminder template, the `ledger_reminder` row, the permission codename and the acceptance criterion without a single judgement call. That traceability spine is the single most valuable thing in the corpus and it is the thing most likely to be eroded by the first three months of implementation pressure.

**The FRD depth is real, not performative.** I read `LED-01` and `SAL-02` in full specifically to test this, because a twenty-four-section template is trivially easy to fill with restatement. `SAL-02` §11 BR-10 contains a worked three-line invoice with the document-discount allocation computed to the paisa, the residual-to-largest-line rule demonstrated, and the inter-state variant given — `subtotal 1,692.14`, `discount 50.00` allocated `25.26 / 13.51 / 11.23`, `taxable_total 1,642.14`, `cgst 65.02`, `sgst 65.03`, `grand_raw 1,772.19`, `round_off −0.19`. That is a specification an implementer can fail against. `LED-01` §13 EC-8 ("tenant timezone makes today differ from the device clock") is the kind of edge case that is normally discovered in production. The asymmetric CGST/SGST split (`21.38 / 21.37`) is called out as intentional in three separate chapters. This is genuine.

**Business rules are stated as rules, not as behaviour.** `LED-01` BR-2 gives the balance formula *and* names the recompute rule as the source of truth, so a reader knows which one wins when the cache disagrees. `SAL-02` BR-9 states four invariants that are asserted rather than assumed. `BR-13`'s credit-limit algebra names its variables. This is what lets a test be written from the document.

**The Avoid list with reversal triggers (Part 11 §11.3) is the correct shape for a scope document.** Fourteen exclusions, each with a condition that would change the answer, expressed in numbers ("more than 30 % of paying tenants employ a full-time accountant who logs in weekly"). That converts every future scope conversation from a negotiation into a measurement, and Part 11 §11.6's eight ordered tests give the person holding the question a procedure rather than a meeting.

### 41.1.2 Findings

**BA-01 (S1) — Nine normatively referenced chapters do not exist.** Parts 32 through 40 are cited as binding in at least thirty places and are absent from the corpus. The citations are not decorative: Part 12 §12.7 says every cut "is a Class C change and is recorded in Part 39"; Part 18 §18.1 routes all change control through "Part 10 §10.7" and Part 39; Part 13 §13.8 makes phase-boundary revision depend on Part 39 entries; Part 12 §12.8 requires "Part 35's definition of done" as its per-feature equivalent; Part 28 §28.6.2 fails the pipeline "on any unimplemented ID for a feature marked done in the task breakdown" — the task breakdown being Part 32; Part 13 §13.2 asserts a duration derived from "the breakdown in Part 32's sprint plan"; Part 22 §22.1 places the API changelog in Part 40. The consequence is not that documentation is missing. It is that the specification's entire governance mechanism — how a decision is recorded, how a cut is authorised, how a feature is declared done — is a forward reference. Until Parts 32, 35 and 39 exist, there is no procedure for changing this document and no definition of done to gate a feature against, which means the first Class C decision will be made informally and the precedent will hold.

**BA-02 (S1) — The MVP feature count is wrong in three places and right in one.** Part 16 §16.15 states 67 MVP / 41 Phase 2 / 24 Phase 3 / 5 Future. Part 18 §18.6 states 74 / 37 / 19 / 1. Part 12 §12.1 states 74 and explicitly flags the discrepancy as a documentation defect, logging it as OQ-01. Part 13 §13.2 states 74. Part 1 §1.11 — the executive summary, the chapter a stakeholder reads first and the only one they may read at all — states "All 67 MVP features shipped" as a six-month success criterion. OQ-01 is owned by Product and due "before build start", which is correct, but the open question is written as though only Part 16 is affected; the exec summary's success criterion is a contractual number in a document presented for approval and it has not been corrected. A reviewer signing Part 18 §18.16 is signing 74; a stakeholder reading Part 1 believes 67.

**BA-03 (S1) — The error-code registry is incomplete and a test asserts that it is complete.** Part 22 §22.1 enumerates fifteen stable error codes. The FRDs and Part 20 emit at least ten more: `party_archived` (`LED-01` BR-9, which itself says "see CCR-2 for code registration" — CCR-2 is not in the corpus), `kind_not_allowed` (`SAL-02` FR-1), `use_document_void` (Part 20 §20.6.1), `entry_already_reversed` (§20.11.6), `over_allocated` (§20.6.4), `service_busy` (§20.11.2), `impersonation_not_consented` (§20.4.8), `token_stale` (§20.5.1), `invalid_token` (§20.5.2), `low_contrast` (Part 22 §22.3 itself). Part 28 §28.3.6 requires a test asserting "the set of codes the app can emit equals the documented set (no undocumented code, no documented-but-dead code)". That test fails on the first day of implementation, and the natural response — adding codes to the test's allow-list rather than to Part 22 — quietly destroys the contract the frontend is written against (Part 19 §19.4.4's rule: "Code, never message").

**BA-04 (S1) — Five schema change requests are specified in the architecture chapter and absent from the schema chapter, and precedence says the schema wins.** Part 20 §20.12.4 lists CR-BE-1 (`platform_job`), CR-BE-2 (`platform_idempotency_key`), CR-BE-3 (`sales_document.version` / `purchases_document.version`), CR-BE-4 (`platform_membership.permissions_version`), CR-BE-5 (`platform_user.token_epoch`), each marked "Specified here; Part 21 to absorb". `SAL-02` §15 independently flags `version` and the idempotency store as CR-SAL-3. Part 18 §18.1 and Part 19 §19.0 both state that where a chapter and the schema disagree, the schema wins. Part 22 §22.1 *mandates* `version` on mutable documents and 24-hour idempotency-key storage. So the API specification requires columns the schema authority does not contain, the architecture specifies them and defers to the schema, and the schema has not absorbed them. An implementer obeying precedence builds without optimistic concurrency and without idempotency storage — which is to say, without two of canon §0.11's non-negotiable rules.

**BA-05 (S2) — The FRD test IDs do not cover the FRD requirements, and the traceability gate cannot detect it.** Part 28 §28.6.3 defines done as "every `FR-n` has at least one test; every `BR-n` has at least one test; every `EC-n` has at least one test; every `AC-n` is green". `LED-01` has 12 functional requirements, 10 business rules, 10 edge cases and 6 acceptance criteria — 38 items — against 12 listed test IDs (`T-LED-01-1` … `-12`). `SAL-02` has 19 FRs, 21 BRs, 20 ECs and 10 ACs — 70 items — against 23 listed test IDs. Part 28 §28.6.1 states that one executed test may carry several IDs but that this "is discouraged — it makes a failure ambiguous", and says nothing about one *FRD* ID discharging several requirements, which is what would be needed here. The traceability matrix joins FRD test IDs to executed tests; it does not join requirements to test IDs. So the gate detects an unimplemented `T-` ID and is structurally blind to a `BR-` with no `T-` at all. On the product's two most important features, roughly two thirds of the stated requirements have no nominated test.

**BA-06 (S2) — Two normative chapters specify the same background-job registry with different names and different schedules.** Part 20 §20.8.4 presents "the MVP job catalogue (every one of these must exist; nothing else may be enqueued)". Part 29 §29.3.2 presents "Periodic schedule (MVP)". They disagree on names and times:

| Work | Part 20 §20.8.4 | Part 29 §29.3.2 |
|---|---|---|
| Auto reminders | `ledger.schedule_auto_reminders`, daily 08:00 IST | `ledger.reminders_due`, daily 09:30 IST |
| Low-stock scan | `inventory.scan_low_stock`, nightly 01:00 IST + after issue | `inventory.low_stock_scan`, daily 08:00 IST |
| Snapshot refresh | `reports.refresh_snapshots`, hourly | `reports.refresh_snapshots`, every 15 min |
| Attachment GC | `files.gc_orphans`, nightly 02:00 IST | `files.gc`, daily 03:00 IST |
| OTP purge | `platform.purge_otp_challenges`, hourly | `platform.purge_otp`, daily 03:30 IST |

Part 20 additionally forbids enqueuing anything outside its list, which makes Part 29's `platform.verify_hostnames`, `reports.partner_usage`, `platform.reconcile_entitlements`, `reports.expire_exports`, `platform.purge_notifications`, `ops.check_certificates` and `ops.verify_backup` illegal. Neither chapter cites the other. An agent building the registry must choose, and whichever it chooses, the other chapter's tests and runbooks reference names that do not exist.

**BA-07 (S2) — The environment-variable catalogue exists twice, disjointly, and both claim exhaustiveness.** Part 20 §20.13.2 defines forty-odd `UB_`-prefixed variables and states "Anything not in this table must not be read by the code", backed by a startup check. Part 29 §29.2.4 defines a different catalogue with unprefixed names (`SECRET_KEY`, `ALLOWED_HOSTS`, `SMS_BACKEND`, `MEDIA_ROOT`, `PUBLIC_BASE_URL`, `SCHEDULER_INTERVAL_SECONDS`) and states it "is asserted complete by the settings test (Part 28 §28.3.9): every variable any settings module reads appears here, and every variable here is read by something". Part 28 §28.3.9 names Part 29 as the authority. The settings module names also differ: Part 20 uses `config.settings.dev` / `.prod` / `.test`; Part 29 defaults `DJANGO_SETTINGS_MODULE` to `config.settings.local`; Part 28 §28.3.9 imports `config.settings.production`. The scheduler interval is 15 s (Part 20), 60 s (Part 29) and 60 s (Part 18 NFR-12). Two of the three chapters that a deployment engineer reads will be wrong whatever is built.

**BA-08 (S2) — The query-budget table exists twice with different numbers.** Part 20 §20.14.1 budgets `GET /parties` at 4 queries, `GET /parties/{id}` at 5, `GET /sales/invoices` at 5, `GET /sales/invoices/{id}` at 6, `GET /reports/dashboard` at ≤8, and any write endpoint implicitly by composition. Part 28 §28.3.10 budgets "simple list (parties, items, expenses)" at 7, "list with aggregates in `meta.totals`" at 9, "document detail with lines, party, payments" at 11, "dashboard" at 14, "any write endpoint" at 25. Part 28 also declares the budget "a ceiling that may only be lowered". Both are normative; the looser one will be the one the test asserts, so Part 20's tighter budgets are decorative.

**BA-09 (S2) — Coverage thresholds disagree across three chapters.** Part 12 §12.5 and Part 18 NFR-45: backend line coverage ≥ 80 % overall, ≥ 95 % on money/stock services. Part 28 §28.7: "Overall gates: backend ≥ 85 % line, frontend ≥ 75 % line", with per-layer floors. Part 19 §19.14.6: jest floor 80 % statements on the pure layers, "no floor on components", against Part 28's 70 % component floor. Release criterion R4 says "coverage thresholds met" without naming which. A release gate whose threshold is ambiguous is not a gate.

**BA-10 (S2) — Performance targets for the same measurement differ across four chapters.** The client-side page-load target appears as: FCP ≤ 2.5 s on 3G-equivalent (Part 18 NFR-08); LCP ≤ 2.5 s on mobile 3G Fast (Part 19 §19.9.1); LCP ≤ 2.5 s / TTI ≤ 3.5 s on mid-range 4G and ≤ 4.0 s / ≤ 6.0 s on low-end 3G (Part 28 §28.9.1 P6). FCP and LCP are different metrics; "3G" and "3G Fast" are different throttles; the device tier is unqualified in two of the three. The ledger-entry target appears as ≤ 8 s median open-to-saved (Part 18 G2, `LED-01` §1), ≤ 250 ms P95 server time (NFR-01, `LED-01` §5, Part 20 §20.14.6) and "< 400 ms tap to persisted ledger entry on a 2 GB Android device" (Part 1 §1.11, restated as Part 28 §28.9.1 P1). The last of these is the only one a merchant experiences and it is the only one not in Part 18's NFR table.

**BA-11 (S2) — Part 19 §19.13 attributes the testing strategy to Part 30, which is Observability.** "Part 30 owns the test strategy; this section owns only the architectural affordances" and, in the table, "Page journeys | E2E | Playwright (Part 30; not in the frontend dependency list…)". Testing is Part 28. A reader following the reference lands on logging.

**BA-12 (S2) — The E2E suite's feature-ID mapping is wrong in at least five places, and three specs test Phase 2 features.** Part 28 §28.5.3: E27 maps the cashbook to `RPT-03` (sales register; day book is `RPT-02`); E28 maps the day book to `RPT-03`; E29 maps the GST summary to `RPT-06` (stock summary; GST summary is `RPT-07`); E26 maps the UPI QR to `PAY-04` (receipt; QR is `PAY-03`). E17 tests `SAL-14` (customer invoice page — Phase 2), E34 tests `WLB-03` (partner hostname — Phase 2) and its seed fixture requires "a verified `*.localhost` host", which is `WLB-03` machinery. Since the traceability matrix is built by parsing these IDs (§28.6.2), the matrix will report coverage for features that were not tested and no coverage for features that were.

**BA-13 (S2) — The state-inventory requirement is inconsistent between the FRD template and the testing chapter, and the product's flagship feature does not satisfy either.** Part 28 §28.4.5 requires every feature's primary components to be tested for "Initial, Loading, Empty (all three variants), Success, Error, Disabled, Partial, Processing, Completed, Failed" — twelve states counting the empty variants. `LED-01` §9's States table has ten rows and its final row reads "Processing / Completed / Failed | SMS status is not shown in this flow", i.e. three of the required states are declared not applicable on the most-used screen in the product. Part 12 §12.8 separately requires "every screen has its three empty states (first-use, filtered-empty, error) and its loading and error states". Three different state inventories, one of which the reference feature does not meet.

**BA-14 (S3) — The Django application list is inconsistent between canon and the architecture.** Canon §0.10 names thirteen backend apps (`platform`, `parties`, `ledger`, `inventory`, `sales`, `purchases`, `payments`, `expenses`, `reports`, `notifications`, `files`, `imports`, `tax`). Part 20 §20.1.1 says "thirteen Django applications" and then §20.1.2 tabulates fifteen, adding `common` and `help`. Table prefixes are derived from app labels (canon §0.10), so the app list and the table-prefix list are supposed to be the same list.

**BA-15 (S3) — The compose topology is four services in canon and six in the deployment chapter.** ADR-019, Part 18 C1 and Part 12 §12.8 all specify `db`, `backend`, `scheduler`, `frontend`. Part 29 §29.1 adds `nginx` in staging and production and §29.2.4 adds a `backup` service with eight environment variables of its own. The backup service carries what Part 29 §29.5.1 calls "the most important operational content in this chapter". A service that important should be in the canonical topology.

**BA-16 (S3) — Idempotency-key scope is stated three ways.** Part 22 §22.1: "per tenant". Part 20 §20.4.6: "scoped to `(tenant, user, endpoint)`". Part 20 §20.6.5: table with `user_id` as "the actor who first used the key" and `U(tenant_id, scope, key)` — i.e. not scoped by user. Part 28 §28.2.6 tests "the same key used by a different tenant is independent" and says nothing about users. The unique index is the real answer; two prose statements contradict it.

**BA-17 (S3) — Two named artefacts hold the shared tax fixture.** Part 19 §19.2.4 specifies `src/tests/fixtures/taxEngine.cases.json` as the file that keeps the Python and TypeScript engines in step. Part 28 §28.10 specifies `tests/fixtures/gst/tax_cases.json` with 120 computed cases as "the single source of truth for both the Python and TypeScript tax tests". Two single sources of truth is none.

**BA-18 (S3) — The state defaulting heuristic in the onboarding journey is not implementable.** Part 15 §15.1 step 4: "State, defaulted from a mobile-number heuristic but editable." Indian mobile numbers have not carried usable geographic information since mobile number portability in 2011. This is a one-line journey detail with a downstream consequence: `platform_tenant.state_code` is the supplier state that decides CGST/SGST versus IGST on every invoice (`SAL-02` BR-11), so a wrong default is a wrong tax split on the first invoice of any merchant who does not correct it.

### 41.1.3 Questions I would need answered before signing

1. Which count is the MVP — 67 or 74 — and who updates Part 1 §1.11 and Part 16 §16.15 once it is decided?
2. Are Parts 32, 35 and 39 going to exist before the first commit? If not, what is the interim procedure for recording a cut and declaring a feature done, and who holds it?
3. Which chapter owns the job registry, the environment catalogue, the query budgets, the coverage floors and the performance budgets — and will the losing chapter be amended or deleted? A cross-reference is cheaper than a duplicate table, every time.
4. Are the five CR-BE items and CR-SAL-3 being folded into Part 21 before the build, or is Part 20 being promoted above Part 21 in the precedence order for those five columns?
5. Does "every `BR-n` has at least one test" mean the FRDs must grow their §21 test lists to cover every rule, or does it mean the executed suite must cover them and the FRD lists are indicative? The two readings differ by roughly 200 tests across the MVP.
6. Who is the compliance owner named in Part 13 §13.4 and Part 18 §18.16? Four approval signatures are required and one of them is a role nobody has been appointed to.

### 41.1.4 What I would add

- **A machine-checked consistency suite over the specification itself**, run in CI alongside the code: every feature ID referenced anywhere exists in Part 16; every error code used in an FRD or in Part 20 exists in Part 22 §22.1; every table and column named in an FRD §15 exists in Part 21 §21.3; every `T-` ID in an FRD §21 is unique; every chapter cross-reference resolves to an existing chapter and section. Eight of the eighteen findings above are mechanically detectable and would have been caught before this review.
- **A requirement-to-test matrix at the FRD level**, not only a test-ID-to-executed-test matrix. The column that matters is "FR-7: which test?", and it is the column that does not exist.
- **A single normative-table index** at the front of the corpus: one row per normative table (job registry, environment catalogue, error codes, query budgets, coverage floors, performance budgets, permission matrix, status values), naming the one chapter that owns it. Every duplicate table in this corpus exists because two authors each needed the table in front of them.
- **A "filed period" rule.** `SAL-02` FR-14 permits issuing an invoice dated in the previous financial year, and EC-10 confirms it allocates from that year's sequence. Part 15 §15.8 addresses voids changing a filed period and is silent on new documents doing so. The product needs either a block, a warning, or an explicit statement that amending a filed period is the merchant's business — but it needs one of them written down, because Vikram Joshi's single stated churn trigger is a revised filing.
- **A data-dictionary section in Part 21** listing every column referenced by an FRD §15 that Part 21 does not define. I found `version`, `credit_days`, `sms_opt_in`, `consent_source`, `consent_at`, `permissions_version` and `token_epoch` in that category; a mechanical pass would find more.

### 41.1.5 Verdict

**Approve with conditions.** The requirements themselves are of a quality I would normally have to write myself. What is not acceptable is that the corpus contains at least eight pairs of contradictory normative tables and a governance mechanism that lives in nine chapters which do not exist. Conditions, in order:

1. BA-01 discharged: Parts 32, 35 and 39 exist in at least skeletal, usable form before the first commit, or an interim procedure is written into Part 10 and signed.
2. BA-02, BA-03, BA-04 discharged: the feature count reconciled everywhere including Part 1; the error-code registry completed in Part 22; the five CR-BE items and CR-SAL-3 absorbed into Part 21 or Part 21's precedence explicitly amended.
3. BA-06 through BA-10 resolved by nominating an owning chapter per table and reducing the others to cross-references.
4. BA-05 answered as a policy decision, with the consequence costed either way.

Everything else I would accept as a defect backlog against a named editorial owner.

## 41.2 The Product Manager

### 41.2.1 What is right

**The MVP definition is the best thing in the document.** Part 12 §12.1 does not define the MVP by feature count or by time-box; it defines it as "the smallest product with which a real Indian small business can stop using paper for its udhaar, its bill book and its stock register on the same day, and never need to go back to them", and then derives three consequences that actually bind: parity before novelty, depth is where the cut happens rather than breadth, and config-gated features ship complete. That definition does work. It is why `LED-03` (correction) is in the MVP and `INV-10` (camera barcode) is not — a merchant can stop using paper while typing a barcode and cannot stop using paper without a way to fix a mistake. Most MVP definitions I am handed are a list with a date attached; this one is a decision procedure.

**The cut-line policy (Part 12 §12.7) is agreed before the pressure arrives, ordered, and honest about what each cut costs.** Ten items, first to last, each stating what is lost and what the merchant does instead, plus a never-cut list that includes the engineering rules rather than only features. The detail that convinces me the author has done this before is item 9: `PLT-10` (export and deletion) is on the cut list "solely so nobody cuts it silently", with the cut conditioned on a documented manual process. That is what a real cut-line policy looks like.

**The phase themes are commercial propositions, not feature groups.** "Replace the paper", "Serve the staffed business and the partner", "Automate compliance and open the verticals". Each phase has entry criteria, exit criteria and a stated commercial rationale for its position, and Part 13 §13.8 makes revision happen at boundaries with typed evidence. The rule that "movement is an exchange, not an addition" is the single most useful sentence in the roadmap.

**The dependency order (Part 13 §13.7) is right and is stated as testable claims.** Platform before everything; parties before ledger; ledger before documents; inventory before sales; tax rates before any document; purchases before valuation; payments before aging; notification adapters before any caller. The reasoning given for "ledger before documents" — that building documents first produces either documents that post nothing or a ledger designed around documents, inverting the spine — is exactly the failure mode the whole product thesis exists to avoid.

### 41.2.2 Findings

**PM-01 (S1) — The differentiation claim and the monetisation claim are the same feature on opposite sides of the paywall.** Part 11 §11.2 lists "Staff roles without a desktop licence" as a defensible differentiator, with the reasoning that competitors gate multi-user behind paid desktop tiers and therefore cannot open it without losing the revenue line that justifies their valuation. Part 1 §1.9 then states that UdhaarBook's own revenue comes from "a paid tier priced per business per year with user-count steps, whose wall is drawn exactly where the research says willingness to pay appears: multiple devices and users with roles, inventory and GST billing depth, server-sent messaging". Both cannot be true. Either staff roles are the free differentiator that competitors cannot copy, in which case they are not the paywall and the monetisation thesis has lost its primary lever; or staff roles are the paywall, in which case the differentiator is "our multi-user tier is cheaper than theirs", which is a price claim, not a defensibility claim, and it is copyable in one pricing-page edit. Part 18 §18.12's activation metric "≥ 20 % invite a second member in 30 days" is measuring the same event as the conversion event, with no statement of which it is. This is the central strategic question in the product and the corpus states both answers.

**PM-02 (S1) — `PLT-15` enforces plan limits at MVP and no plan limits exist.** `PLT-15` (plan entitlements) is an MVP feature: "Plan → module and limit map (users, parties, invoices/month) with enforcement hooks". Part 21 §21.3.1 gives `platform_plan.limits jsonb` with `{max_users, max_parties, max_invoices_per_month, storage_mb}`. `SAL-02` §12 gates issue on `max_invoices_per_month` with `403 plan_limit_reached`, EC-17 requires it to fire before any write, and Part 28 §28.2.7 has a concurrency test for two devices creating the 300th party at limit 300. Meanwhile OQ-02 — "Commercial model: flat annual per business with user-count steps, monthly subscription, or per-active-merchant to partners — and what is included free?" — is owned by Product and due at **Phase 2 entry**, i.e. after the MVP has shipped. So the MVP ships enforcement of limits whose values are undecided, on a free tier whose shape is undecided, and Part 1 §1.9 simultaneously promises "no entry caps" while `max_parties` and `max_invoices_per_month` are the two caps the schema carries. A kirana with 250 credit customers hits `max_parties` on day one of migration if that number is set conservatively, and the migration is the whole adoption thesis.

**PM-02a (S2, corollary).** The free tier as described in Part 1 §1.9 — "parties, entries, statements, reminders, UPI QR, one user, one business, no ads, no entry caps" — includes `LED-06` reminders and `PAY-03` UPI QR, both of which Part 11 §11.1 lists as table stakes. It excludes nothing that a single-shop kirana needs. Suresh Sharma, the first persona, never becomes a paying customer under this design, and he is 80 % of the addressable base by count. That may well be correct — he is the acquisition engine and the partner channel's volume — but nowhere is it stated as a deliberate decision, and the 12-month target of "5 % free-to-paid on self-serve" is stated as 2.5× the category benchmark without naming which persona converts.

**PM-03 (S1) — Phase 2 is 37 features gated on an event nobody owns.** Part 13 §13.3's entry criteria require "a named partner with a signed intent, because `WLB-03`–`WLB-06` built speculatively will be built wrong". I agree with the rule. The consequence is unmanaged: if no partner signs, four of Phase 2's thirteen feature groups are blocked, the phase's exit criterion ("One partner live on its own hostname… with at least ten of its merchants transacting") is unreachable, and the release train's 2.1 milestone at launch + 16 weeks has no contents. There is no stated alternative Phase 2. Part 18 §18.14 names "partner dependency" as a risk and mitigates it by *not building speculatively*, which manages the waste and not the schedule. The roadmap has no branch.

**PM-04 (S2) — The sequencing of `PLT-15` (MVP), the commercial model (Phase 2 entry) and `PLT-16` self-serve billing (Phase 3, launch + 50 weeks) means revenue is collected manually for roughly a year.** Phase 2's exit criterion requires "a paying cohort… revenue is collected from real merchants or from a partner per active merchant" at roughly launch + 22 weeks. `PLT-16` arrives at launch + 50 weeks. For 28 weeks somebody raises invoices, chases renewals, applies entitlement changes and handles failed payments by hand, for a target of 300 paying tenants at 12 months. Nobody is named for that work, no tooling is specified for it beyond `PLT-14`'s "entitlement overrides", and `PLT-14` is the first item on the cut list.

**PM-05 (S2) — The differentiation list contains one item that is not defensible and one that is not a differentiator.** Part 11 §11.2's "One book instead of four apps" is claimed as an architectural commitment competitors cannot retrofit. The argument is that a ledger app adding invoicing "bolts a documents table onto a transactions table and gets two balances". That is true of a ledger app. It is not true of `myBillBook` or Vyapar, which already have documents, lines, tax and a receivables view; for them the work is a label, an entry screen and a party-first navigation, not an architecture. The corpus contains a detailed competitor analysis (Parts 4, 9) and no competitive-response analysis anywhere: there is no scenario in which an incumbent ships a khata tab in month six, no statement of what UdhaarBook does then, and no identification of which differentiators survive that. Separately, "GST that knows its own history" (rate effective dates) is described in the same section as "low technical difficulty, high consequence" — which makes it table stakes that competitors have got wrong, not a differentiator. It belongs in §11.1.

**PM-06 (S2) — The 24-month revenue target implies a conversion rate five times the 12-month target and ten times the category benchmark, with no explanation.** Part 1 §1.11: at 12 months, 5,000 tenants created, 1,500 monthly active, 300 paying — 20 % of active, 6 % of created. At 24 months, 30,000 created, 10,000 monthly active, 2,000–2,500 paying — 20–25 % of active, 7–8 % of created. So the ratios hold; what does not hold is the relationship to the stated benchmark of "~2 % category conversion" and the stated self-serve target of 5 %. The resolution is presumably that the partner channel converts at a much higher rate because the partner pays per active merchant rather than the merchant paying — but the targets do not separate the two lines, and "partner-sourced revenue ≥ 30 % of total" at 12 months implies 70 % is self-serve, i.e. 210 self-serve paying tenants out of 1,500 active = 14 % self-serve conversion, not 5 %. Two numbers in the same table disagree by a factor of nearly three.

**PM-07 (S2) — The single metric the document names as most important is not instrumented.** Part 1 §1.11: "The single number that matters most across all three horizons… is the share of active tenants using both the ledger and at least one document module in the same month." Part 18 §18.12's metric table has a "Depth" tier with "Invoice adoption — GST-registered tenants issuing ≥ 1 invoice in 30 days ≥ 35 %" and "Second member invited", neither of which is the stated north star. The actual north star row in §18.12 is "Weekly transacting tenants — tenants posting ≥ 5 ledger entries **or** ≥ 1 document in a week", which is a union where the thesis needs an intersection. Part 31's metric tree would be the place to fix this and I would want to see it there before launch.

**PM-08 (S2) — The pilot's exit criterion cannot be evaluated on the product the pilot uses.** Part 12 §12.8 and Part 18 R10 require "at least five of ten [merchants] report at day 45 that they no longer maintain the paper khata". Part 11 §11.1 names "customer-side transparency" — the SMS a customer receives when something is written against their name — as "the single most-praised behaviour in the category… it makes disputes disappear". `LED-08` is config-gated and inert without a DLT-registered provider, and Part 12 §12.8 accepts launch with "DLT registration either complete or the channel provably inert". So the pilot that decides whether the paper khata is abandoned may run entirely without the feature the research says is the strongest driver of ledger trust. If the answer comes back below five, we will not know whether the product failed or the channel was off.

**PM-09 (S2) — The roadmap has no capacity model, so the phase durations are unfalsifiable.** Part 13 §13.2 gives Phase 1 as "20–24 weeks with a team of four to six engineers" and allocates 5 + 5 + 4 + 5 + 3 = 22 weeks by module. That allocation covers the 74 features and nothing else. It does not contain the design-system workstream (Part 23 §23.7 nominates "Sprint 1–2" for roughly thirty `Ub*` components each with a test and a gallery entry, plus four print templates), the 35-spec E2E suite, the three in-house performance harnesses, the traceability tooling with three runner integrations, the two hand-written QR encoders, the hand-written service worker, the contrast checker, the analytics pipeline and sink endpoint, the CSV import framework, or `PLT-14`. Part 18 A10 acknowledges that if the BrandHub conventions are not reusable "the design-system and frontend-architecture estimates in Part 13 are understated by several weeks" — but there is no line in Part 13 for a design-system estimate to be understated against.

**PM-10 (S3) — Two roadmap features contradict the Avoid list's own reasoning.** Part 11 §11.3 item 7 excludes lending and states that `LON-01`–`LON-03` are "a *daily-collection loan book* for merchants who already lend — a bookkeeping feature". Part 13 §13.4 then schedules them in Phase 3 with an entry criterion of "a merchant segment that already lends on daily collection", and OQ-12 asks "Does `LON` belong in this product at all, or in a partner's?" — owned by Product, due at Phase 3 planning. A module with its own three features, its own tables and a place in the release train, whose existence is an open question, is a scope commitment made by inertia. Either delete it from the roadmap until the trigger is met, or answer OQ-12 now.

**PM-11 (S3) — `EXP-03` (cashbook) is fourth on the cut list and is the only "accounting" the target persona does.** Part 11 §11.1 says of expenses and cashbook: "Not accounting — a day-wise view of cash and bank in and out with opening and closing figures, which is the only 'accounting' most owner-operators do." Part 12 §12.7 then cuts it fourth, on the reasoning that expenses still appear in the day book. The day book is a chronological list of everything; the cashbook is the closing figure the merchant counts against the drawer at 9:30 p.m. They are not substitutes, and journey 3 step 8 ends the merchant's day on the dashboard's "cash in hand" tile, which is the cashbook's number.

### 41.2.3 Questions I would ask before approving

1. Is multi-user free or paid? Answer that one sentence and half of Part 11 §11.2, Part 1 §1.9, `PLT-15` and the §18.12 metric table need editing.
2. What are the free tier's limits, numerically, and do they include `max_parties`? If `PLT-15` must ship in the MVP, the numbers must exist before the MVP, which moves OQ-02 from Phase 2 entry to before build start.
3. If no partner signs by week 12 of Phase 1, what is Phase 2? I want the alternative phase written now, while it is cheap, not negotiated in week 22.
4. What is the response if Khatabook or OkCredit ships GST billing, or Vyapar ships a free ledger tier, during Phase 1? Name the differentiators that survive each, and the ones that do not.
5. Which of the 74 MVP features would I cut to fund the design system, the E2E suite and the eleven in-house utilities that Part 13's 22 weeks does not contain? If the answer is "none, the estimate absorbs it", I want the absorbed estimate.
6. Is the day-45 pilot question asked with `LED-08` live or inert, and if inert, what do we conclude from a "no"?

### 41.2.4 What I would add

- **A partner-less Phase 2.** Thirteen weeks of `INV-10`–`INV-15`, `PUR-05`–`PUR-08`, `RPT-09`–`RPT-12`, `HLP-01`–`HLP-03` and `PAY-06`–`PAY-07` is a coherent, sellable phase that needs no partner. Write it as the default and make the partner phase the variant, because the variant is the one contingent on somebody else's signature.
- **A competitive-response section in Part 13**, three scenarios deep: incumbent ledger app adds billing; incumbent billing app adds a free khata tier; a payments platform bundles a free ledger with its soundbox. For each, the differentiators that survive, the ones that do not, and the earliest indicator we would see.
- **A pricing page, written as a page, in Part 18.** Not the commercial model — the page, with the tier names, the wall, the numbers and the renewal commitment. Part 12 §12.8 already requires "Pricing published and the renewal commitment stated" as a launch gate; a launch gate whose artefact does not exist eight chapters before launch is a gate that will be waived.
- **One instrumented north-star metric**, defined as the intersection Part 1 §1.11 names, with its query, its dashboard and its weekly review owner — replacing the union currently in §18.12.
- **A "what we will not measure" list.** The metric table has fifteen rows and a team of five. Three of those rows will be looked at weekly and the rest will be looked at when somebody asks. Name the three.
- **A turnover-threshold warning.** `RPT-07` already computes outward supplies for the period. A tenant approaching ₹5 crore of annual turnover should be told, in-product, that e-invoicing will apply and that `SAL-12` is Phase 3 — because A5 says that tenant "cannot legally use the product for B2B invoices" and currently the only mechanism for telling them is a sales conversation that will not happen.

### 41.2.5 Verdict

**Approve with conditions.** The scope discipline, the MVP definition and the dependency order are better than anything I would produce unaided, and I would defend the cut list unchanged. But the product currently has two incompatible answers to "how does it make money", ships limit enforcement with no limits, and has a second phase contingent on a signature nobody holds. Conditions:

1. PM-01 resolved in writing: multi-user is free or paid, and every affected chapter amended.
2. PM-02 resolved: free-tier limit values fixed before build start, or `PLT-15` reduced at MVP to module entitlement only with limits deferred to Phase 2 and the enforcement hooks left dormant.
3. PM-03 resolved: a partner-independent Phase 2 written and adopted as the default plan.
4. PM-09 resolved: Part 13's Phase 1 estimate re-derived with the non-feature workstreams included, and either the duration or the scope moved to match.
5. PM-07 and PM-08 acknowledged with owners: the north-star definition corrected, and the pilot protocol stated with `LED-08`'s status recorded against each merchant's day-45 answer.

## 41.3 The UX Designer

### 41.3.1 What is right

**The vocabulary decision is the whole product.** Canon §0.2's vernacular mapping — "You gave" = उधार दिया, "You got" = जमा, "Hisaab" = statement, "Baaki" = balance — and product principle P4 ("The user's words, not accounting's") do more for adoption than any interaction design in the corpus. Journey 1 step 7 puts it where it matters: the opening-balance field is labelled "इनसे लेना है / इन्हें देना है", not "debit/credit". A product that asks Suresh Sharma to understand debit and credit has already lost, and this one never does.

**Progressive disclosure is applied with an actual rule, not as a slogan.** Part 19 §19.2.2 fixes it mechanically: "if the form has line items, it gets a route; otherwise it gets a drawer". Every three-field interaction — ledger entry, payment, expense, party, stock adjustment, reminder — is a drawer over the list with no route change; every document is a full-screen route with deep links, drafts and back semantics. That single rule removes a hundred individual arguments and produces the right answer in each of them.

**The eight-second budget is treated as a design constraint rather than a performance aspiration.** `LED-01` §5 and §7 put the amount field pre-focused with the keypad already up, the Save button pinned in the drawer footer above `env(safe-area-inset-bottom)`, no confirmation dialog, no fetch on drawer open, and "Save & add another" as a first-class action. Part 15 §15.3 step 3 notes that "nothing has cost him a decision yet". That is a designer thinking about a man with a customer waiting, and it is the reason the product might survive its first week.

**The correction experience is designed, not merely permitted.** Journey 12 is the best piece of writing in the corpus: the merchant is being watched across the counter while he fixes his own mistake, the reversal and the replacement are both visible with the reason, and the statement is re-shareable with the trail. Part 11 §11.4's "approval workflows reduced to reason capture" delivers accountability through `UbReasonDialog` with a plain-language consequence line ("Stock +2, Ledger −₹898") instead of an approval chain nobody in a thirty-square-metre shop would use. Designing the embarrassing moment into a trust moment is the highest-value UX decision here.

**Empty states are treated as findings.** Part 23 §23.1 carries Koper's rule — "empty states name the gap and offer the one action that closes it" — and Part 14 §14.11 goes further: a module irrelevant to a persona "must be genuinely absent, not merely empty", because "an inventory tab showing 'no items yet' to a kirana owner who sells no tracked goods is a permanent reminder that the product is not for him".

### 41.3.2 Findings

**UX-01 (S1) — The uppercase label tier cannot render Hindi, and it is used for every table header and badge in a product that must be 100 % translated.** Part 23 §23.2.2 defines `ds-label` as 11 px, weight 600, letter-spacing `+0.09em`, **UPPERCASE**, and §23.3 assigns it to "Card micro-labels, table headers, badges" and to the label inside `UbField` ("an 11 px uppercase label" — Part 19 §19.5.4) and `UbStatCard` ("label: string — 11px uppercase"). Devanagari has no case, so `text-transform: uppercase` is a no-op on Hindi strings, which means every table header renders at a visibly different weight and rhythm between locales. Worse, `+0.09em` tracking breaks Devanagari conjuncts and matra positioning, and 11 px is below the size at which matras are legible on a 2 GB phone's screen at arm's length in a shop. Part 19 §19.11.6 states the rule correctly — "`ds-label` (line-height 1.25) is only ever used for short uppercase Latin labels" — and Part 23 assigns it to the field labels of every form in the product. Both cannot stand. Every form label, every table header and every stat-card label in the product is a Hindi string.

**UX-02 (S1) — The design system's own contrast audit fails the accessibility requirement it states.** Part 23 §23.6 reports "success on white 3.9:1 → success used only ≥ 14 px medium or with icon". Part 23 §23.2.4 assigns `--success` to "'You got', paid, in stock", and §23.3/`LED-01` §7 render credit amounts as `text-success` inside `UbTimeline` rows and `UbDataGrid` cells, whose type ramp is `ds-body-sm` at **13.5 px**, weight 400. That is below the size and weight the chapter's own exception permits, and 3.9:1 fails WCAG 2.2 AA (4.5:1) for text under 18.66 px. NFR-25 requires "axe-core with zero critical violations on every screen". Separately, `--text-muted` is `#8A94A0` on `--canvas` `#F6F7F9` — roughly 2.3:1 — and §23.2.4 restricts it to "Labels only, never body"; but Part 19 §19.12.4 mandates that every error surface larger than a field message renders the request id "in `ds-mono`, `ds-caption` size, `text-text-muted`", which is 12.5 px body text at 2.3:1, on the screen a distressed user is trying to read.

**UX-03 (S1) — The offline behaviour specified in Part 19 contradicts the offline behaviour specified in `LED-01` and journey 3, and the difference is the product's most important interaction.** Part 19 §19.10.3 (degraded-mode UX at MVP): "Every **write** affordance is disabled with an inline hint 'You are offline. This will work again when you have signal.'" `LED-01` FR-12 and Part 15 §15.3's poor-network block specify the opposite: the user taps Save, an optimistic row appears labelled "सेव हो रहा है…", and on failure it turns amber with Retry while the drawer's contents are preserved. Both behaviours are reachable in the same session, because `useOnlineStatus()` is a `navigator.onLine` listener plus a 30-second health probe, and the shop's actual failure mode — the shutter half down, the signal present but dead — is precisely the case where `navigator.onLine` reports true and the request times out. So the merchant experiences the disabled-button path when the browser knows it is offline and the optimistic path when it does not, with no design covering the transition. The optimistic path is the correct one and it is the one grounded in the journey; the disabled path is honest but it takes the "You gave" button away from a man standing at a counter, which Part 14 §14.5 identifies as the exact moment Sunita reaches for the paper bill book.

**UX-04 (S2) — The receivable red collides with the error red, the destructive red and the overdue red, in the same viewport.** Part 23 §23.2.4 maps `--error` to four meanings at once: "'You gave'/receivable, overdue, out of stock" plus every validation error and every error banner. In the `LED-01` drawer, the drawer's top accent bar is `--error` (because the direction is debit), the Save button is primary blue, and a validation message under the amount field is also `--error` — so the colour that means "this is a healthy receivable" and the colour that means "you have made a mistake" are the same colour, six centimetres apart. §23.3's choice to render destructive buttons as *outlined* danger shows the author saw part of this; the amount-text and field-error collision is not addressed. The research requires red for udhaar and that must not change; what must change is the error treatment.

**UX-05 (S2) — Colour is paired with words but not with sign, and the words are the thing that gets truncated.** NFR-25 and P4 commit that "colour is never the sole carrier of meaning — red and green always accompany the words 'You gave' and 'You got'". In `UbTimeline` (`LED-01` §7) the row's primary line is "note or 'You gave'/'You got' label" — so when the merchant has typed a note, which is the common case and the one journey 3 demonstrates ("चीनी 10 किलो"), the words are replaced by the note and the only remaining differentiator is the tone of the amount. `UbAmount`'s specification (Part 19 §19.1.2) says it "formats a decimal string with a tone" and nothing about a sign prefix. Roughly 8 % of Indian men have a red-green deficiency; the timeline is the screen they look at most.

**UX-06 (S2) — Touch targets are specified at 44 px and the default button size is 36 px.** NFR-26 and Part 23 §23.6 require "≥ 44 × 44 px for primary actions" and "touch targets ≥ 44 px on mobile". Part 23 §23.3 maps `MLButton` sizes to "sm 30 / md 36 / lg 44". Nothing states that mobile must use `lg`, and the components most likely to be hit with a thumb are icon buttons and row menus: the `⋯` row menu (`SAL-02` §7), the mobile line-item stepper "− 1 +", the date chips, the payment-mode chips (six of them in a row at 360 px), and `UbFilterTag`'s remove affordance. `SAL-02` §7's mobile line card also specifies "swipe-left to remove" with no accessible or keyboard equivalent, and Part 28 §28.5.4 asserts the 44 px rule only "by measuring the bounding box of primary controls".

**UX-07 (S2) — The ten-state requirement is stated three times with three different inventories and no feature in the corpus satisfies the largest one.** Part 12 §12.8: "every screen has its three empty states (first-use, filtered-empty, error) and its loading and error states" — five. The FRD template's §9: a table of ten named states. Part 28 §28.4.5: "Initial, Loading, Empty (all three variants), Success, Error, Disabled, Partial, Processing, Completed, Failed" — twelve. `LED-01` §9 fills ten rows, of which one is "Not applicable (form)" and one collapses three states into "SMS status is not shown in this flow". The cost is not the rows; it is the copy. Twelve states × 74 features × 2 languages, plus every error message and every empty state's action label, is well over a thousand strings, and Part 19 §19.11.3 makes a missing key a **build failure** (`check-locales.mjs`). Every state we specify and do not write copy for is a red build discovered by an engineer at 6 p.m., who will then invent the Hindi. That is how a product's voice dies.

**UX-08 (S2) — The invoice editor is designed for a persona who is not the one Part 14 says matters most, and there is no reduced mode for the one who is.** `SAL-02` §7's desktop layout has eleven line-grid columns, a place-of-supply control, a reverse-charge switch, a document-discount control, a Rule 46 checklist panel, a sticky totals panel with seven rows, and an eleven-key function-key map (`F2`–`F8`, `Ctrl+S`, `Ctrl+Enter`, `Ctrl+P`, `Ctrl+N`). That is a correct and rather good design for Ramesh's billing clerk on a Windows desktop with a keyboard-wedge scanner. It is not a design for Sunita Kumari on a shared Android tablet — Part 14 §14.5 says she is "the person who uses the product most", reads "Roman-script Hindi faster than Devanagari", and is "slow at anything that changes" — nor for Suresh, for whom Part 14 §14.11 scores Sales as priority 3. The mobile layout is specified and is decent, but it is the same document model with the same fields, and there is no "quick bill" path: two items, one total, cash, print. `SAL-07` (walk-in) is the closest thing and it is a variant of the same editor.

**UX-09 (S2) — The onboarding is four questions and one of them cannot be answered by its own default.** Journey 1 step 4 and `PLT-03` ask business name, business type, GST status and state, with state "defaulted from a mobile-number heuristic". That heuristic has not worked since number portability. The state field is not cosmetic: it is `platform_tenant.state_code`, which `SAL-02` BR-11 uses to decide CGST/SGST versus IGST on every invoice the tenant will ever raise, and `SAL-02` FR-5 uses as the final fallback for place of supply. A merchant who leaves the default gets the wrong tax split. The design fix is small — default from the GSTIN when one is entered (positions 1–2, which `SAL-02` EC-2 already relies on), and otherwise make the state a required selection with a searchable list rather than a pre-filled guess — but the journey currently reads as though the guess is a kindness.

**UX-10 (S2) — The service-worker cache stores party names and balances for 24 hours on a device the spec knows is shared.** Part 19 §19.10.2 caches `GET /api/v1/parties`, `/items`, `/auth/me`, `/reports/dashboard` and the first page of `parties/{id}/ledger-entries` stale-while-revalidate with a 24-hour TTL. Part 20 §20.14.5 sets `Cache-Control: no-store` on everything for a stated reason: "a shared browser on a shop counter must not leak the previous user's data from the back button". Part 14 §14.5 says Sunita works from "a shared Android tablet at the counter". The service-worker cache is keyed by tenant and cleared on logout and tenant switch, which handles the multi-tenant case and not the multi-*person* case, and `PLT-11` (app lock / PIN) is Phase 2. Invoice drafts in `localStorage` (Part 19 §19.5.7) carry party names, mobiles and amounts under the same conditions.

**UX-11 (S3) — There is no specified behaviour for the Hindi expansion of the three widest strings in the product.** Part 19 §19.11.6 correctly notes Hindi runs 15–25 % longer and forbids fixed-width buttons. The three places where that bites are not designed: the `UbStatCard` label at 11 px uppercase on a 360 px dashboard with two tiles per row ("आपको मिलने हैं" against "TO COLLECT"); the six payment-mode chips in a single row in the `LED-01` drawer; and `UbStatusBadge`, whose specification says "Uppercase pills carrying state, never counts" with statuses like `partially_paid` → "आंशिक भुगतान". A wrap rule, a two-line allowance or an abbreviation list is needed for each.

**UX-12 (S3) — The reminder queue is honest and slow, and the honesty is worth keeping.** Journey 4 step 5 presents "8 में से 1" with Send and Skip because `wa.me` opens one chat at a time. That is the right decision and it should be stated as a design principle rather than a workaround, because the same shape recurs anywhere a platform limit meets a bulk action. What is missing is the interrupted-queue state: the merchant will abandon at 5 of 8, and nothing specifies whether the queue resumes, how the three unsent parties are shown, or whether the three `ledger_reminder` rows were written at selection time or at send time (§15.4 step 7 implies one row per party, step 6's failure-mode note says "the message is built at send time").

### 41.3.3 Questions I would ask before approving

1. Is `ds-label` uppercase Latin-only? If so, what is the Hindi label tier, and does it change the vertical rhythm of every form and table?
2. Which red is the receivable red and which is the error red? I need two, and I need the second one to not be used for money.
3. When the network is unreliable but not absent, does the "You gave" button work or is it disabled? One answer, applied everywhere.
4. Who writes the Hindi copy, when, and against what inventory? A native reviewer is named in Part 19 §19.11.7 step 5 but not scheduled, and the copy is a build gate.
5. Is there a quick-bill mode for the kirana and the counter staff, or is the document editor the only billing surface at MVP?
6. On a shared counter tablet, what is the boundary between two staff members' sessions before `PLT-11` exists?

### 41.3.4 What I would add

- **A locale-aware type scale.** One extra token set: `--size-label-hi: 12.5px`, `--track-label-hi: 0`, no `text-transform`, applied by `:lang(hi)`. Roughly fifteen lines of CSS that makes every form label and table header legible in the product's primary language.
- **A signed `UbAmount`.** A `+`/`−` prefix (or an arrow glyph) in addition to tone and label, so the timeline is readable without colour and without the label the note displaces. This is the cheapest accessibility improvement available in the product.
- **A distinct error treatment that is not red text on money screens.** Outlined error borders are already the rule (§23.5); extend it: field errors render as an outlined message block with an icon, and reserve red *text* for amounts.
- **A copy inventory as a deliverable, owned by design, delivered before the module it serves.** One sheet per feature: every label, every empty state (three), every error message by error code, every snackbar, every `aria-label`, in `en` and `hi`. Part 19 §19.11.7 describes the workflow; nothing schedules the artefact. The i18n build gate makes this the critical path for every feature, not a polish task.
- **A "counter mode" for `SAL-02`**: party optional, one search field, quantity steppers, a single total, cash/UPI toggle, issue-and-print. Same service, same document, one screen. It is the screen Sunita and Suresh will use and it is the screen that is not specified.
- **A specified degraded-network state machine** with three states, not two: online, *uncertain* (requests failing but `navigator.onLine` true), and offline. The uncertain state is the real one and it currently has no design.
- **An interrupted-bulk-action pattern**, derived from the reminder queue and applied to imports, exports and multi-select actions generally.

### 41.3.5 Verdict

**Approve with conditions.** The interaction design, the vocabulary and the trust choreography are excellent and in several places better than anything shipping in this category. The design *system*, however, contains two accessibility defects that its own chapter documents (the success-on-white ratio and the muted-text ratio), a label tier that does not work in the product's primary language, and an offline story that contradicts itself on the product's most frequent action. Conditions:

1. UX-01 and UX-02 fixed in Part 23 before the design-system sprint starts, since every component inherits them.
2. UX-03 resolved with a single specified behaviour, written into both Part 19 §19.10.3 and `LED-01` FR-12.
3. UX-07 resolved as a copy-inventory commitment with an owner and a schedule, because the alternative is that engineers write the Hindi.
4. UX-04, UX-05 and UX-06 accepted as design-system amendments before the first `Ub*` component is built.
5. UX-09 fixed in `PLT-03` — it is a four-line change with a tax consequence.

## 41.4 The Frontend Architect

### 41.4.1 What is right

**The layering rule is stated as law with an exhaustive import table, and it is enforceable.** Part 19 §19.1.2 gives, per layer, what may be imported and what must never be, with "may import" declared exhaustive, backed by `import/no-restricted-paths`. The two load-bearing rules — no component ever imports axios or a service, and no `Ub*` component knows about the domain — are the right two, and the single documented exception (the public document view) is called out inline. I have worked in three codebases that had this rule in a wiki and none that had it in an ESLint config; this one has it in the config.

**The worked data flow for `LED-01` (§19.1.3) is the most useful page in the chapter.** Tap → hook → thunk → optimistic row keyed by `clientId = idempotencyKey` → service → transport → fulfilled → cross-feature `extraReducers` on `partySlice`. Three architectural properties are then named explicitly: the idempotency key is minted once in the hook and lives in the slice so a remount cannot re-mint it; cross-feature updates go through `extraReducers` on the other feature's action so the dependency graph stays acyclic; and the snackbar is dispatched by the component because thunks are UI-free. Those are the three mistakes a team makes in month two, pre-empted.

**The optimism policy is correct and unusually disciplined.** §19.3.7: optimistic in exactly three places — ledger entries, tab switches, reversible toggles — and pessimistic for everything that allocates a number, moves stock or changes history, with the reason stated ("A shopkeeper must never be told an invoice exists when it does not"). Most teams get this backwards and optimistically render the invoice.

**Token storage is right and the reasoning is right.** §19.7.1 chooses httpOnly cookies over BrandHub's readable bearer token, and justifies it by the actual threat: the product renders user-entered party names across every screen, print templates are exactly where teams reach for raw HTML, and one `dangerouslySetInnerHTML` would exfiltrate a readable token. The refresh single-flight queue's five requirements in §19.4.3 are the complete set, including the two everybody forgets (`/auth/refresh` never triggers a refresh; a 401 *after* a successful refresh is not retried).

**The build order in §19.14.7 is the correct order** and it is the thing an agent needs most: utilities and types, then tokens and Tailwind, then transport, then store, then i18n and validation, then the `Ub*` subset, then the shell, then `parties` end-to-end as the reference implementation, then everything else.

### 41.4.2 Findings

**FE-01 (S1) — The design-system path contradicts itself and canon within the same chapter, and every import in the codebase depends on it.** Canon §0.10 specifies `src/modules/UdhaarBook/design-system/Ub*/` with a barrel `index.ts`. Part 19 §19.1.2's import table, §19.2.3's feature example and §19.8.2 rule 9 all use `modules/UdhaarBook/design-system`. Part 19 §19.2.1's repository tree places it at `src/design-system/`, §19.2.5's heading is `src/design-system/`, and §19.8.3 puts the token files at `src/design-system/tokens/` while Part 23 §23.7 deliverable 1 calls them `tokens/`. Part 23 §23.1 says the wrappers live in `src/design-system/`. This is a one-word decision and it is wrong in four places; an agent that resolves it the way the trees say will produce a barrel that no feature import resolves against, and the failure appears only after several hundred files exist.

**FE-02 (S1) — The invalidation map is normative, incomplete, and references slices the repository tree does not define.** §19.3.6 is explicitly the section "that replaces what a query library would have given us", with the instruction: "when you add a mutation, you add a row here and the corresponding `extraReducers` cases." The table has thirteen rows. The MVP has 74 features. Missing mutations include, at minimum: `voidPayment`, `voidInvoice`'s effect on `paymentList`, `issueCreditNote`, `applyCreditNote`, `recordPurchaseBill`, `voidPurchaseBill`, `postOpeningBalance`, `writeOffBalance`, `correctEntry`'s effect on `partyStatement` (listed, but `partyStatement` is not a slice in §19.2.1), `saveReminder`, `sendReminder`, `commitImport` (which creates parties and ledger entries wholesale and therefore invalidates almost everything), `saveTenantSettings` (which changes numbering, due days and tax defaults and therefore invalidates every editor's defaults), `saveMembership` (role change → permissions → every view-model's action list), `uploadBranding` (theme), `archiveItem`, `saveCategory`, `saveUnit`, `saveExpenseCategory`. Three of the thirteen existing rows name targets that do not appear anywhere as slices: `reports.*`, `partyStatement`, `partySearchCache`, `itemSearchCache`. An implementer must invent both the slices and the missing rows, and every omission is a stale screen that nobody notices until a merchant reports a number that "did not update".

**FE-03 (S1) — Part 19 and Part 28 give opposite instructions about the client tax engine.** Part 19 §19.2.4: "`view-model/taxEngine.ts` is a mirror, not a source of truth… Its unit tests use the *same fixture table* as the backend's `test_tax_engine.py`, checked in at `src/tests/fixtures/taxEngine.cases.json`, so a divergence fails CI on both sides." Part 28 §28.4.8, in the table of what is deliberately *not* unit-tested on the frontend: "Tax and total arithmetic — The server is authoritative (canon rule 3); the client's numbers are previews. Covered instead by: Backend money tests; one E2E asserting the displayed total equals the server's." These are contradictory instructions to the same engineer about the same file. They also disagree on the fixture path (§28.10 puts 120 cases at `tests/fixtures/gst/tax_cases.json`). I hold Part 19's position: an untested client tax engine that runs on every keystroke in the product's most scrutinised screen will diverge, and the merchant will see one total and be charged another, which is the exact failure `SAL-02` §1 calls "zero tolerance".

**FE-04 (S2) — Redux without a server-state library is a defensible choice whose cost is under-stated by about an order of magnitude, and the chapter says so without pricing it.** ADR-004 and constraint C6 forbid TanStack Query on the ground that "one pattern, consistently applied, is worth more than a better pattern applied twice", which is a real argument. What the pattern costs here, concretely: every list feature needs a slice, a thunk, a service, a hook, selectors and a row in the invalidation map — §19.3.4 says the party list is "the reference implementation… Every other list feature is a transliteration of these three files", and there are roughly eighteen lists in the MVP. Every mutation needs its `extraReducers` cases in *every* slice it touches (`issueInvoice` touches eight). Request deduplication, abort-on-filter-change, mount-time refetch policy, staleness-by-age (three slices with TTLs), pagination in two modes and optimistic rollback are all hand-written and specified across §19.3.4 through §19.3.8 — which is roughly what a query library's README contains. I am not asking to reverse ADR-004. I am asking that the cost be acknowledged as approximately 1,500–2,500 lines of infrastructure plus a permanent review obligation on the invalidation map, because that number belongs in Part 13's estimate and it is not there.

**FE-05 (S2) — The `/dashboard` bundle budget is smaller than the chart library it contains.** §19.9.2 budgets the `/dashboard` route chunk at 55 KB gzipped for "tiles + the one chart bundle", and §19.9.3 says charts are `next/dynamic` via `ml-uikit`'s recharts wrappers. Recharts with its d3 dependencies is materially larger than 55 KB gzipped on its own — it is the single largest thing in the frontend dependency set — so either the dashboard chart is not recharts, or the chart's dynamic chunk is excluded from the route budget (which the table does not say), or the budget fails on the first PR that adds the chart. Related: the shared app chunk at 85 KB must hold Redux Toolkit, react-redux, axios, react-intl, dayjs, `cn` and "the `Ub*` set used by the shell" — the shell's `Ub*` set is `UbAppShell`, `UbSidebar`, `UbBottomNav`, `UbFab`, `UbSnackbar`, `UbPageHeader`, `UbPageShell`, `UbSkeleton`, plus their Radix primitives (dialog, sheet, popover, toast). That is achievable but has no headroom, and §19.9.2's only stated consequence of exceeding a budget is that the build fails, with no policy for what happens next.

**FE-06 (S2) — The `Ub*` wrapper layer is thirty-plus components with a ten-point construction checklist, a test, a gallery entry and a barrel line each, and no chapter budgets it.** Part 23 §23.7 deliverable 2 lists the MVP subset: PageShell, PageHeader, DataGrid (five files), Tabs, StatCard, Dialog, ConfirmDialog, ReasonDialog, Drawer, Field, MoneyInput, DateInput, DateRangePicker, PhoneInput, Combobox, AsyncCombobox, StatusBadge, EmptyState, Snackbar, Skeleton, BottomNav, Fab, Sidebar, LineItemsEditor, TotalsPanel, PartyHeader, Timeline, QrCode, ShareSheet — plus `UbAmount`, `UbCard`, `UbForm`, `UbFileUpload`, `UbFilterTag`, `UbHelpHint`, `UbPercentInput`, `UbQuantityInput`, `UbSearchInput`, `UbStatusBanner` from §19.2.5's tree. That is ~38 components. Part 19 §19.8.2 requires ten specific properties of each, Part 23 §23.4 requires a `*.test.tsx` and a gallery row, and Part 28 §28.4.6 specifies what each test must cover. At a genuinely fast two components per day including tests, that is four engineer-weeks before a single feature screen exists, and Part 13's 22-week allocation has no line for it. The maintenance burden is the second half: every one of these is a wrapper whose only job is to hold product defaults over an `ML*` primitive, and §19.8.5's promotion rule ("only once the pattern is used by two or more features") is the right rule but is contradicted by Part 23 §23.7, which mandates the whole set as a sprint-1–2 deliverable before any second use exists.

**FE-07 (S2) — The line-item editor is the hardest component in the product and its specification is split across three chapters that each assume the others.** `SAL-02` §7 gives eleven columns with pixel widths, an eleven-key keyboard map, a scanner burst detector (≥ 8 chars in < 50 ms), per-line inclusive/exclusive toggles in a cell addon menu, a row context menu, and a mobile card form with steppers and swipe-to-delete. Part 19 §19.5.5 gives the three performance rules (memoised rows keyed by `field.id`, one `Controller` per editable cell, `useWatch` scoped to `lines`). Part 23 §23.3 lists it as a new shared component with the guideline "Keyboard-first counter". Nothing specifies: focus restoration after a row is deleted, what happens to focus when a scanner fires while a qty cell is focused, the undo-toast's interaction with `useFieldArray` indices, how the 5-second undo window interacts with autosave, whether `Alt+↑/↓` increments a quantity with three decimal places by 1 or by 0.001, or what the editor does at 200 lines when §19.9.4 has ruled out virtualisation. It is also, per §19.9.1, expected to repaint totals within 50 ms at 20 lines on a 2 GB phone while running a `decimal.js-light` tax engine over every line on every keystroke.

**FE-08 (S2) — `UbDataGrid` must render two ways from one column definition, and only one way is specified.** §19.2.5 lists `UbDataGridMobileList.tsx` — "the < md card rendering of the same columns" — and §19.9.2 makes it load-bearing for the bundle budget: "`@tanstack/react-table` is loaded only by routes that render `UbDataGrid`. Mobile list rendering uses `UbDataGridMobileList`, which renders the same column definitions as cards without the table core; on a phone the table engine never ships." That is a good idea whose mechanics are not stated. TanStack column definitions carry `cell` renderers that take a `CellContext` produced by the table core; rendering them without the core means either re-implementing enough of the context to satisfy the renderers, or declaring a parallel card shape per column, which is what teams actually end up doing. And the conditional import must be resolvable at build time to keep the table core out of the mobile bundle, which a runtime viewport check does not achieve.

**FE-09 (S2) — Two hand-written QR encoders exist for the same payload.** Part 23 §23.3 specifies `UbQrCode` as a "New shared… tiny local generator, no external service". Part 20 §20.10.3 specifies "a small vendored QR encoder in `apps/common/integrations/upi/qr.py` (byte mode, error-correction level M, no external dependency, ~200 lines, unit-tested against known-good fixtures for three payloads)" serving `GET /payments/qr.svg`. Both render the same UPI intent string, both are outside ADR-021's allow-list as first-party code, and a divergence between them means the QR on the client-rendered invoice and the QR on the server-served statement scan to different strings. Neither chapter mentions the other.

**FE-10 (S2) — The public document view breaks the chapter's own two hardest rules and the exception is one sentence.** §19.1.2: "The only exception in the entire codebase is the public document view (§19.7.6), which is unauthenticated, has no store, and calls one service function directly from its page content — documented inline as such." §19.1.4 makes it the one genuine server component. That page must also render the invoice print template (which is a React print component sharing tokens with the app), apply tenant branding resolved from the share token, render the UPI QR, and support its own `/print` route. So the one exception to the layering rule is also the surface a customer sees, the only server-rendered page, the only place tokens are applied without `whiteLabelSlice`, and a component-sharing boundary with the print templates. It needs a section, not a sentence.

**FE-11 (S3) — `react-error-boundary` is on the allow-list and the App Router's own `error.tsx` files do most of the work.** §19.12.1 uses both: `app/error.tsx` and `app/(app)/error.tsx` for the two page tiers, and `<UbErrorBoundary>` (built on `react-error-boundary`) for the component tier. The component tier is used in four named places. That is a dependency for four call sites of a thirty-line component, in a chapter whose second consequence is "the third-party dependency list is closed".

**FE-12 (S3) — Mount-time refetch policy and the 60-second dashboard TTL can show a merchant a stale number immediately after they change it.** §19.3.6 marks `dashboard` stale on `postLedgerEntry`, so the dashboard refetches on next mount — and Part 22 §22.11 plus Part 20 §20.14.5 let the *server* serve a `reports_snapshot` refreshed hourly (Part 20) or every fifteen minutes (Part 29) for tenants above the 5,000-entry threshold. The client's invalidation is therefore defeated by the server's snapshot for exactly the tenants who transact most. Journey 3 step 8 ends the merchant's day on that dashboard.

### 41.4.3 Questions I would ask before approving

1. `src/design-system` or `src/modules/UdhaarBook/design-system`? One answer, applied to canon, Part 19 and Part 23 in the same edit.
2. Is the client tax engine tested against the shared fixtures, or is it untested by policy? Part 19 and Part 28 must not both be true.
3. Who owns the invalidation map, and is a missing row a review blocker? If it is not, the map decays to documentation within two months.
4. What is the policy when a bundle budget fails — split, defer, or raise the budget with a justification? "The build fails" is not a policy.
5. Does the dashboard chart ship as recharts? If so, the `/dashboard` budget needs a number that includes it.
6. Is the `Ub*` set built ahead of the features (Part 23 §23.7) or on second use (§19.8.5 rule 2)? The two rules produce different sprint 1s.

### 41.4.4 What I would add

- **A `createListFeature` factory.** Eighteen list features that are "a transliteration of these three files" is eighteen opportunities to transliterate incorrectly. A typed factory producing the slice, the thunk, the mount-refetch hook and the standard selectors, parameterised by the service function and the row type, is roughly 200 lines and removes the transliteration entirely. It is compatible with ADR-004 because it is our own code, not a library.
- **The invalidation map as code, not as a table.** One module exporting `INVALIDATION: Record<ThunkName, { patch?, stale: SliceKey[], refetch?: SliceKey[] }>`, consumed by a single store-level listener middleware, with a type that fails to compile when a slice key does not exist and a test that fails when a mutating thunk has no entry. That converts FE-02 from a documentation risk into a compile error, and it is the single highest-value addition I would make to this architecture.
- **A `useDegradedNetwork()` three-state hook** (online / uncertain / offline) shared by the shell strip, the write affordances and the retry policy, resolving UX-03 in one place instead of per feature.
- **A frozen component contract test** for `UbDataGrid`: one column definition, asserted to render identically in table and card modes, so FE-08's parallel rendering cannot silently diverge.
- **One QR implementation.** Put it on the server (`GET /payments/qr.svg` already exists), have `UbQrCode` render an `<img>` at that URL with a client-side fallback only for the offline print case, or make the client the only implementation and have the server embed the same SVG string. Two is the only unacceptable number.
- **A performance budget for the line editor expressed as a test**, not a target: React Profiler assertion that a qty keystroke at 20 lines causes ≤ 2 row re-renders (§19.9.6 already proposes this — promote it from "measurement plan" to a CI gate, because it is the one component where a regression is invisible until a counter queue forms).

### 41.4.5 Verdict

**Approve with conditions.** This is a good architecture that an agent can build from, and the layering, the optimism policy and the auth design are all correct. Three things must be fixed before the first commit because they are cheap now and expensive later: the design-system path (FE-01), the tax-engine testing contradiction (FE-03), and the invalidation map's completeness mechanism (FE-02, ideally by making it code). The `Ub*` workstream (FE-06) must appear in the plan with a number attached. And I want it recorded that ADR-004's cost is real and is being paid deliberately — in month six the thing that will hurt is not Redux, it is the fourteenth time someone forgets a row in the invalidation map and a merchant sees a balance that "did not update", which is the one bug this product cannot afford to have.

## 41.5 The Backend Architect

### 41.5.1 What is right

**The immutability design is correct and is defended in depth.** Append-only `ledger_entry` and `inventory_stock_movement`; no `UPDATE`/`DELETE` path in application code; `http_method_names` on the viewset excluding PUT/PATCH/DELETE; a `forbid_update_delete` trigger permitting only `status` and `reversed_by_id`; an AST check that no module calls `.update(` or `.delete(` on those models; and a test that queries `pg_trigger` after the full migration run so a trigger lost to a later migration is caught (Part 28 §28.2.4 T-IMM-9). Four independent mechanisms for one invariant is the right number for the one invariant whose violation is unrecoverable.

**The service/selector split is the right CQRS for this product and the rules that make it stick are stated.** Rule D6 (services never import DRF), D7 (views never import another app's models), D8 (selectors never write) and D9 (signals may only do things that are safe to lose) are the four rules that keep a Django monolith from becoming a ball of mud, and D9's reasoning — "a balance that depends on a signal is a balance that silently breaks when a service uses `bulk_create`" — is exactly right. §20.3.1's hard rule ("every business behaviour must be reachable without HTTP") is what makes `LED-02` FR-6's claim that the CSV importer calls the same `post_opening_balance()` true rather than aspirational.

**Lock ordering is declared globally, which almost nobody does.** §20.11.2's L0 chain plus the six numbered rules, specifically L2 (sort lines by `item_id` before locking) and L3 (lock documents in the same order FIFO visits them), are the two that prevent the deadlocks this product would otherwise get. The concurrency test plan in §20.11.6 tests eight named races with real threads against real Postgres, including `test_lock_order_no_deadlock` with two threads issuing documents with the same two items in opposite line order.

**The `platform_job` design is a genuinely good queue for this scale.** `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` with a CTE-plus-`UPDATE … RETURNING` claim in one round trip; visibility timeout with lease reaping; attempts incremented at claim so a job that kills its runner still dead-letters; enqueue-time dedupe via a partial unique index on `idempotency_token`; `scheduled_key` making recurring work identical to ad-hoc work downstream; and the observation in §20.8.3 that enqueuing *inside* the transaction is strictly better than `transaction.on_commit` because on-commit work is lost if the process dies. That last point is the kind of thing you only know after losing some.

**The rounding table (§20.7.3) settles every computation by name, and rejects banker's rounding with a reason.** Sixteen rows, each with the formula, the quantisation and the mode, plus the explicit statement that SGST is derived from `total_tax − cgst` and never rounded again — which is why CGST+SGST always equals the total tax. Two implementations that round differently diverge over thousands of movements; this table is the reason they will not.

### 41.5.2 Findings

**BE-01 (S1) — The weighted-average cost cache cannot be reproduced by the replay the spec says reproduces it, whenever a purchase is backdated.** §20.6.2 states: "`recompute_item_stock` is a pure replay: iterate movements in `(movement_date, created_at, id)` order, apply the same function, and assert the final pair equals the cache." The incremental path applies movements in *arrival* order, because `post_movement` updates `inventory_item_stock.avg_cost` when the row is written. Weighted average is order-dependent. Part 15 §15.6's failure-mode block explicitly permits backdating — "Goods arrive before the bill — at MVP the answer is to record the bill when it arrives and backdate it" — and `SAL-02` FR-14 permits documents dated into the previous financial year. The moment a purchase bill dated the 3rd is entered on the 9th, after sales and purchases on the 5th and 7th have already moved the average, the incremental `avg_cost` and the replayed `avg_cost` differ, permanently. Part 28 §28.2.5's stock recompute test asserts "a random sequence of 500 movements replayed equals the incrementally maintained cache to the paisa" and §28.2.3 asserts the formula over "a 1,000-movement sequence" — neither requires backdated movements in the fixture, so the suite is green and the invariant is false. NFR-40 ("nightly recompute finds zero drift") and Part 12 §12.5's stock-drift launch gate would then fail in production on the first tenant who backdates a bill, and the merchant's stock *valuation* — `INV-08`, `RPT-06` and the Phase 2 profit summary — is wrong either way. This is the most serious defect I found.

**BE-02 (S1) — Voiding a purchase bill does not restore the weighted-average cost, and journey 6 promises that it does.** §20.6.2: "outbound movements never change the average — `next_average_cost` returns `avg_cost` unchanged for `qty <= 0`". A void posts reversing movements (`reverse_movements_for_source`, "opposite movements with `reverses_id`"), and the reversal of an inbound is an outbound, which by the stated rule leaves `avg_cost` untouched. So voiding the purchase removes the quantity and keeps the cost effect. Part 15 §15.6's failure-mode block states the opposite as a user-facing promise: "voiding the bill (`PUR-04`) reverses both the stock movements and the ledger entry **and recomputes the average**; the product never patches an average in place." Recomputing is exactly right and is the only correct answer — but §20.6.2 and `PUR-04` do not call `recompute_item_stock`, and if they did, BE-01 means the recomputation itself is order-sensitive. The concrete scenario is the one journey 6 names: a cost mistyped by a factor of ten, voided, and the average silently left ten times too high for every subsequent valuation and every subsequent weighted-average update.

**BE-03 (S1) — The product's central correctness guarantee has no scheduled job in either chapter that schedules jobs.** NFR-40 requires "nightly recompute finds zero drift between denormalised and derived values", G3 makes zero drift a goal, Part 12 §12.5 makes it a pass/fail launch gate, and §20.11.5 specifies `check_invariants` as running "nightly as `platform.check_invariants`; a non-empty report is an operator alert". `platform.check_invariants` does not appear in §20.8.4's job catalogue, which states "every one of these must exist; nothing else may be enqueued". `parties.recalc_balances_for_tenant` does appear, described as "operator / post-incident", not nightly, and there is no `inventory.recalc_stock_for_tenant` at all. Part 29 §29.3.2's periodic schedule — the other normative list — has fifteen rows and no drift, invariant or recompute entry. So the nightly job that Part 18, Part 12 and Part 20 §20.11.5 all rely on is not scheduled anywhere, and the alert that depends on it (Part 30 §30.10, `invariant.violated`) has nothing to fire from.

**BE-04 (S1) — Part 29's global advisory lock defeats Part 20's concurrent-runner design and serialises the queue behind its slowest job.** §20.8.5 makes `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` "the whole concurrency story: any number of runners may run simultaneously and each claims a disjoint batch without blocking". Part 29 §29.3.3 then wraps the entire tick in a PostgreSQL session advisory lock on one integer key, "held for the tick's duration", so that "a second scheduler container, an accidental `--loop` in a terminal, or a cron tick overlapping a slow previous tick" is harmless. Part 29 §29.3.2 step 4 executes each claimed job *inside* the tick. Therefore: one runner, and everything queued waits behind the current batch. Part 28 §28.9.3's own scenarios make this concrete — a 50,000-row sales-register export "completes ≤ 90 s" and an import commit of 5,000 rows "≤ 60 s"; either of those, claimed into a batch, holds the advisory lock and blocks the reminder dispatch, the transaction SMS, the low-stock scan and every other tenant's work for a minute and a half. Part 28 §28.9.1 P9 then requires "scheduler drains a 1,000-job backlog in ≤ 60 s", which is unachievable under a serialising lock with `--batch 50` unless every job averages three seconds. The two chapters describe different systems.

**BE-05 (S2) — Lock rule L4 inverts the global lock order it belongs to, and overrides an FRD by assertion.** L0 fixes the order `platform_tenant → platform_document_sequence → parties_party → sales_document → payments_payment → inventory_item_stock → ledger_entry`. L4 then says the sequence is "locked after the tenant row (if any) and before any party or stock lock, and held for the shortest possible span — allocate the number late in the issue, not first. (`SAL-02` BR-16 lists sequence allocation as step 1 for *logical* clarity; the implementation allocates after validation and stock checks have passed, which is the same observable behaviour and holds the hottest lock for less time. **This refinement is normative.**)" If the stock *check* is a read, the order holds. But `SAL-02` BR-16 step 3 is "stock check + `sale_out` movements", and §20.6.2's `post_movements` "takes the item lock in a deterministic order (rule L2)" — so a check-then-allocate-then-write sequence either takes the `inventory_item_stock` locks before the sequence lock (violating L0) or performs an unlocked read whose result is stale by the time the number is allocated (permitting the over-issue that `test_concurrent_issue_same_stock` exists to prevent). Separately, Part 10's precedence places the FRD above the architecture chapters for behaviour, and §20.11.2 declares its own refinement normative over `SAL-02` BR-16 without a change request. Whichever is right, one of them must be edited rather than out-voted in a parenthesis.

**BE-06 (S2) — Plan-limit enforcement puts a `FOR UPDATE` on the tenant row on the hottest write path.** `SAL-02` §12 gates issue on `max_invoices_per_month` with `403 plan_limit_reached`, EC-17 requires it "before any write", and Part 28 §28.2.7's "Plan limit overshoot" test asserts that two devices creating the 300th party at limit 300 produce exactly one success "(PLT-15 BR-7, **tenant row locked**)". L0 puts `platform_tenant` first in the lock order, so the tenant row is locked at the start of the issue transaction and held through sequence allocation, stock locks, ledger write, payment write and audit. Every invoice issue in a tenant therefore serialises against every other write that touches the tenant row — including every party create. With `lock_timeout` at 5 seconds (§20.11.2) and a `503 service_busy` on expiry, a counter rush of twenty invoices in sixty seconds from three concurrent sessions (Part 28 §28.9.3) is exactly the shape that produces user-visible lock waits. The limit counts can be satisfied without locking the tenant row — a conditional insert, a counter row per `(tenant, period)`, or accepting a one-over-limit race on a soft limit — and the choice should be made deliberately rather than inherited from a test's parenthesis.

**BE-07 (S2) — The idempotency store has an `in_progress` state whose HTTP response is undefined and whose error code is undocumented.** §20.6.5 gives `status ∈ {in_progress, completed}`. Part 28 §28.2.6 asserts, for two simultaneous requests with the same key, "exactly one creates, the other replays **or gets the in-progress 409**". Part 22 §22.1 documents exactly one idempotency error code, `idempotency_conflict`, defined as "same key + different body". So a concurrent duplicate — the single most common real case, a double-tap on Save over a slow link — returns a 409 with either an undocumented code or a code that means something else, and Part 19 §19.4.4's rule is that the client branches on code. §19.10.4's Phase 2 offline queue then treats `409 idempotency_conflict` as "the write already landed with different content; mark `conflict`… surface it in a 'Not synced' list", which is precisely the wrong handling for a still-in-flight first attempt.

**BE-08 (S2) — Two normative chapters disagree on whether migrations run at container start.** Part 20 §20.12.3: "`docker/entrypoint.sh` runs `python manage.py migrate --noinput` before starting gunicorn, and the `scheduler` waits for the `backend` service, so migrations run exactly once even with several backend replicas (Postgres's migration advisory lock handles the race)." Part 29 §29.5.2: "Migrations are an **explicit, ordered step**, never a container-start side effect", with three stated reasons, the second being that "a failed migration must stop the deploy with a clear error, not produce a crash-looping container that masks the cause", and §29.6.2's release order is build → migrate → restart backend → restart scheduler → restart frontend. §20.13.5 additionally has the entrypoint run `seed_reference_data` on every start. Part 29 is right and Part 20's entrypoint is the thing that will actually exist, because it is in the Dockerfile.

**BE-09 (S2) — The per-connection statement timeout is shorter than the export the performance suite requires.** `UB_DB_STATEMENT_TIMEOUT_MS` defaults to 15,000 (§20.13.2) and is a per-connection setting in `DATABASES`, which the scheduler inherits. Part 28 §28.9.3 requires a 50,000-row sales-register export to "stream, complete ≤ 90 s, memory flat" and a 5,000-row import commit in ≤ 60 s. §20.14.2 prescribes `.iterator(chunk_size=…)` for "anything that walks a whole tenant's history (recalc commands, exports, `check_invariants`)", which holds a server-side cursor on one statement for the duration of the walk. Either the long-running jobs need their own connection with a raised timeout — which nothing specifies — or the recompute commands and the exports will be killed at fifteen seconds on any tenant large enough to matter.

**BE-10 (S2) — Tenancy is enforced only in the application at MVP, and the one mechanism that would make it structural is deferred without a trigger.** Part 21 §21.1 principle 1 and ADR-008 reserve PostgreSQL row-level security for Phase 2, "designed so RLS can be switched on without changes", and §20.4.1 is admirably honest: "The application is therefore the **only** thing standing between tenant A and tenant B at MVP." The compensating controls are strong — `TenantManager` failing closed, `TenantPrimaryKeyRelatedField` making foreign ids indistinguishable from non-existent ones, a generative endpoint sweep, a list sweep, an AST check for `.objects.all()` and unscoped `filter(`, and the `for_tenant(None).count() == 0` assertion. What is missing is a trigger: no chapter states the condition under which RLS moves from "Phase 2, optional" to "required" — the obvious one being the first partner whose security review asks, which is also the first moment it is too late to add cheaply. `reports` is the app most likely to breach this, because §20.1.4 grants it "any app's `selectors/` and models" and reports are where raw SQL gets written.

**BE-11 (S2) — The modular monolith's boundaries have one declared cycle, one app with universal read access, and no rule for the first thing that will violate them.** Rule D5 breaks the genuine `sales ↔ payments` cycle with a documented deferred import, and states "No other cycle is permitted. If a second cycle appears, the design is wrong and a CR is raised." The second cycle is already visible: `imports` imports `parties`, `inventory` and `ledger` services (D4's exception table), and `IMP-01`'s commit path must create parties *with opening balances*, which is `parties.services` calling `ledger.services`, which is fine — but `PLT-10`'s tenant export must read every app, and it lives in `platform`, which D3 forbids from importing a business app at module level. `reports` is granted read access to everything and forbidden from importing any `services/`, yet `RPT-08`'s background export writes `reports_export` rows and `platform_job` results, so `reports` does write — through its own models, which the rule permits by omission rather than by statement. These are all resolvable; none is resolved.

**BE-12 (S2) — `platform_job.tenant_id` is `ON DELETE RESTRICT` and one of the jobs deletes tenants.** §20.8.2 gives `tenant_id` as "FK `platform_tenant` **NULL**, `ON DELETE RESTRICT`", and §20.8.4 registers `platform.delete_tenant` with "token `tenant_delete:{tenant_id}`" — so the job row for the deletion references the tenant it is deleting. Part 21 §21.1 principle 10 says tenant deletion "is a background job that deletes children in dependency order after export". The job cannot delete its own row while it is running and cannot leave it, so `platform.delete_tenant` must either null its own `tenant_id`, delete `platform_job` rows last from outside its own row, or run with `tenant_id = NULL` and carry the id in the payload. §20.11.3 already notes that the deletion job is "the only place" permitted to `DISABLE TRIGGER`; it needs the same explicitness here.

**BE-13 (S3) — `LED-10`'s guarantee has an exception that is not stated where the guarantee is.** Product principle P1 and `LED-10` commit that "every document — invoice, purchase bill, payment, credit note, party-linked expense — posts a ledger entry that links back to its source". `SAL-02` EC-5 (100 % document discount, free sample) says: "Ledger entry skipped because `amount > 0` constraint; audit notes `zero_value: true`." That is the right implementation and it is a documented hole in a rule that Part 18 states without qualification.

**BE-14 (S3) — The round-off `CHECK` admits a value the formula cannot produce.** §20.11.4: `CHECK (round_off >= -0.50 AND round_off <= 0.50)`. `SAL-02` BR-8 and §20.7.3: `round_off ∈ [−0.50, +0.49]`. Harmless, but the constraint should encode the rule it is defending.

**BE-15 (S3) — `Party.balance` is described as both a cache and a lock target with no statement of what happens when they disagree.** `LED-01` BR-2 names the recompute formula as "the source of truth"; EC-7 serialises concurrent posts with `SELECT … FOR UPDATE` on the party; §20.11.5's `recalc_balances` reports drift and only fixes with `--fix`. What is not specified is the behaviour of the *product* when drift is found: does the merchant see the cached balance or the derived one, is the party flagged, is the tenant notified? The nightly job is an operator tool and the merchant is the one looking at the number.

### 41.5.3 Questions I would ask before approving

1. Is the weighted average maintained incrementally in arrival order or recomputed in movement-date order? Answer that and BE-01, BE-02 and the meaning of `avg_cost_after` on every movement row all resolve together.
2. Does `PUR-04` (void purchase bill) call `recompute_item_stock`? If yes, at what cost on an item with 50,000 movements, and inside or outside the void transaction?
3. Where is the nightly drift job registered, and what does the operator alert contain?
4. One runner or many? If one, remove `SKIP LOCKED` from the story and state the queue's serialisation honestly, including what happens to a 90-second export. If many, remove the advisory lock and shard the periodic-task enqueue instead.
5. What locks does a plan-limit check take, and is a one-over-limit race acceptable on a soft limit?
6. Which connection runs long jobs, and with what `statement_timeout`?
7. What is the trigger that makes RLS mandatory rather than optional?

### 41.5.4 What I would add

- **`inventory_stock_movement.sequence_no`, allocated per `(item, location)` at write time, and a rule that the average is maintained in `sequence_no` order with a recompute triggered whenever a movement is inserted with a `movement_date` earlier than the item's current maximum.** That makes the cache and the replay agree by construction, makes backdating explicit and bounded, and turns BE-01 from a silent divergence into a measurable, testable event. The recompute is per item, not per tenant, so it is affordable.
- **A `stock_cost_recompute` job type** enqueued by that rule and by every void, with an idempotency token of `(item_id, location_id, watermark_date)` so a burst of backdated bills collapses into one recomputation.
- **Backdated movements in every drift fixture.** Part 12 §12.5 already requires the balance dataset to include "backdated, reversed, corrected and voided rows"; the stock dataset must require the same, and the stock fixture must include a backdated inbound *between* two outbounds, which is the case that fails.
- **An explicit long-job connection alias** (`DATABASES['jobs']`) with a higher `statement_timeout` and its own `CONN_MAX_AGE`, used by the scheduler, the recompute commands and the export builders. Three lines of settings; it removes BE-09 entirely.
- **A `scheduler.tick` heartbeat assertion per job type, not per tick.** Part 29's heartbeat proves the runner is alive; it does not prove that `ledger.schedule_auto_reminders` ran today. A `scheduled_key` is written per period — so the query "is there a succeeded row with `scheduled_key = ledger.schedule_auto_reminders:<today>` by 10:00 IST?" is the alert that matters, and it is the one the collection journey depends on. Today, a day on which the job was never enqueued leaves no row, no dead letter and no alert.
- **A one-page "what a support engineer runs" appendix**: the drift queries, the reversal-chain query for one entry, the movement replay for one item, and the job history for one tenant. Arjun Menon's stated goal is to answer "what happened to this entry" in five minutes with evidence, and the SQL for that is scattered across Parts 20, 28 and 30.

### 41.5.5 Verdict

**Approve with conditions, and I will not sign the inventory module without BE-01 and BE-02 resolved.** The ledger design is correct and I would build it as written; the ledger is order-independent, its cache is a sum, and the immutability defences are genuinely good. The stock design has an order-dependent cache with a replay that the specification asserts is equivalent and is not, in a scenario the journeys explicitly permit, with a test suite whose fixtures avoid the case. That is the shape of a defect that ships. Conditions:

1. BE-01 and BE-02 resolved in Part 20 §20.6.2, Part 21 §21.3.6 and `PUR-04`, with a backdating fixture added to Part 28 §28.2.5.
2. BE-03 resolved: the drift and invariant jobs registered in one job catalogue with a schedule and an alert.
3. BE-04 resolved: one concurrency model for the scheduler, in one chapter, with the other chapter reduced to a reference.
4. BE-05 and BE-06 resolved as explicit decisions about lock scope on the issue path, with the FRD amended rather than overridden.
5. BE-07 and BE-08 resolved before the first endpoint and the first deploy respectively.

## 41.6 The Product Engineer

### 41.6.1 The question I was asked

Could someone actually build this from these documents? My answer is yes for about sixty percent of it, no for about fifteen percent, and "yes, but not in the time allowed" for the remainder. Below I separate the three, because the distinction between a specification that is precise and one that merely looks precise is the only thing I can usefully contribute.

### 41.6.2 What is genuinely buildable, and why

**`LED-01` I could build on Monday without asking a question.** Twelve functional requirements naming the component, the field, the default and the storage key (`localStorage.ub.lastPaymentMode`); a validation table with the rule, the English message and the error detail key per field; ten business rules including the credit-limit algebra with named variables; ten edge cases including the timezone one and the orphan-attachment one; the exact API shape of the response object; the Redux slice shape (`{ byParty, entities, draft, posting, lastError }`); and the six acceptance criteria written as given/when/then with real numbers. Part 20 §20.3.3 then walks the same feature through all seven backend layers as the reference implementation. This is the standard the rest of the corpus should be measured against, and roughly thirty MVP features meet it.

**`SAL-02` is buildable and is the best-specified hard thing here.** The tax engine is fully determined: BR-1 through BR-9 give the order of operations, BR-10 gives a worked example to the paisa with the inter-state variant, BR-11 gives the UTGST label map with the five state codes, BR-14 gives the numbering format with the 16-character arithmetic (`prefix ≤ 16 − 7 − padding`), BR-15 maps every Rule 46 requirement to a column with a hard/soft marker, and BR-16 gives twelve ordered side effects. An implementer can write `tax_engine.py` from §11 alone and know when it is right.

**The platform layer is buildable.** Tenancy (§20.4), the token scheme with `ver` and `fam` claims (§20.5.1–20.5.2), the permission registry and the three-class ordering (entitlement → quota → authorisation, §20.5.5), the OTP flow with a pepper distinct from `SECRET_KEY`, and the idempotency decorator. These are the parts most likely to be got subtly wrong by an agent and they are the parts specified in most detail.

### 41.6.3 Findings: where the implementer stops and asks

**PE-01 (S1) — Roughly a quarter of the MVP has no FRD depth at all, and the corpus does not say so.** Part 18 §18.6 states that scope is "specified at implementation depth in Part 17 for MVP and Phase 2 features". The four FRD chapters cover PLT, WLB, PTY, LED, PAY, EXP, NTF, INV, PUR, IMP, SAL, RPT and HLP by module heading. What I cannot verify from the headings is per-feature completeness, and the features I would most want to check are the ones with the least natural structure: `PLT-14` (super-admin console), `PLT-15` (plan entitlements), `RPT-01` (dashboard — every tile's query, its freshness and its drill-through), `RPT-07` (GST summary — the HSN summary's grouping rule, the document-series summary's definition of a gap), `IMP-01` (the validate-preview-commit framework, which journey 2 makes the adoption-critical path), and `EXP-03` (cashbook — the opening/closing derivation). Before a build starts, somebody must confirm that all 74 have their §4, §10, §11, §13 and §22, because a feature that is a catalogue row plus a one-line MVP scope is a feature an agent will invent.

**PE-02 (S1) — The eleven things ADR-021 makes us build ourselves are not in anyone's estimate.** The minimal-dependency stance is defensible and I support it on the deployment argument. Its consequence is a second product. Counted from the corpus: a QR encoder on the client (Part 23 §23.3) and another on the server (§20.10.3); a service worker written by hand because Workbox is not on the list (§19.10.2); a contrast checker (`scripts/check-contrast.mjs`, Part 23 §23.7); a bundle-budget checker (`scripts/check-bundle.mjs`); an i18n completeness checker (`scripts/check-locales.mjs`); an i18n extractor; a traceability joiner with a pytest plugin, a Jest reporter and a Playwright reporter (§28.6.2); three performance harnesses (`perf_seed`, `perf_probe`, `perf_explain`) plus `scripts/load.sh` (§28.9.2); a job queue with claim, lease, backoff, dead-letter and a `requeue_job` CLI (§20.8); an idempotency store (§20.6.5); a feature-flag system with deterministic bucketing and a three-level override chain (§31.9.2); an analytics batcher with a server sink endpoint (§19.12.5, §31.6); an HSL ramp generator matched between Python and TypeScript (§19.8.3, Part 24 §24.4.3); a client-side image compressor and a server-side one (§19.9.5, §20.9.3); structured JSON logging with request-id middleware and an access-log APM (§30.2, §30.11); an operations console (§30.8) and the super-admin console (`PLT-14`); and a CSV import framework with row-level validation, preview and chunked commit (`IMP-01`). Individually each is justified. Together, at a realistic estimate, they are eight to twelve engineer-weeks that appear nowhere in Part 13's 22-week allocation, which is allocated entirely to modules.

**PE-03 (S1) — The design-system workstream is a prerequisite for every screen and is scheduled as "Sprint 1–2".** Part 23 §23.7 nominates roughly thirty `Ub*` components for sprint 1–2, each of which must satisfy Part 19 §19.8.2's ten-point checklist, carry a `*.test.tsx` covering render, every variant, the behavioural states, accessible role and name, keyboard operation and `className` merge order (Part 28 §28.4.6), and appear in the internal gallery. Two sprints is four weeks; I would estimate five to seven for one engineer, and every feature screen is blocked behind the subset it needs.

**PE-04 (S2) — The print templates are specified as a design-system deliverable and are actually four of the hardest features in the product.** `SAL-03` governs them, Part 23 §23.7 item 4 lists them as "React print components sharing the same tokens", ADR-014 makes them the entire PDF story, Part 12 §12.8 requires print "verified on one A4 laser and one 80 mm thermal printer physically", and Part 28 §28.4.8 concedes that "print components' pixel layout — not assertable in jsdom" is covered only by "manual print-check on the device matrix". So: four legal documents, rendered by `window.print()` across Chrome, Android WebView, Samsung Internet, Mi Browser and Safari, on two paper sizes, one of which is 80 mm thermal where margins, character width and the QR's quiet zone are all physical constraints, with tenant branding and Devanagari, and no automated test. The 80 mm template in particular has to make a three-line invoice with CGST/SGST columns readable at 32–48 characters per line. This is the single most under-estimated item in the MVP and it is listed as a design-system chore.

**PE-05 (S2) — `RPT-07` (GST summary) has a launch gate that is harder than its specification implies.** Part 12 §12.5 makes "the GST summary reconciles to the sales register to the rupee" a pass/fail launch criterion, journey 8 step 3 has Vikram checking exactly that, and `SAL-02` BR-9's invariants make it achievable — *if* both reports read the same stored document totals, which Part 15 §15.8 asserts they do. The parts that are not specified anywhere I can find: the HSN summary's grouping (by HSN and rate, or HSN alone, and at how many digits given the 4/6/8-digit rule); the document-series summary's definition of "cancelled" (void, or void-plus-deleted-drafts); whether a credit note reduces outward supply in the period of the note or the period of the invoice; whether an invoice backdated into a previous FY (permitted by FR-14) appears in the current period's summary, the previous one, or both; and how a composition tenant's Bill of Supply appears. Each is a rupee-level decision on a gate stated in rupees.

**PE-06 (S2) — Cross-chapter contradictions force the implementer to choose, and there is no mechanism for recording the choice.** From the other roles' findings, an agent building this will hit at least: two job registries with different names (BA-06), two environment catalogues with different variable names (BA-07), two query budgets (BA-08), two coverage floors (BA-09), three performance numbers for the same metric (BA-10), two design-system paths (FE-01), opposite instructions on testing the tax engine (FE-03), two offline behaviours for the Save button (UX-03), two positions on whether migrations run at container start (BE-08), and two scheduler concurrency models (BE-04). Part 20 §20.0 is explicit that "if a decision is not written here, in Part 21, or in Part 22, it is a specification gap and must be raised as a CR, not guessed" — but every one of these is written *twice*, which the rule does not cover, and there is no CR register (Part 39 does not exist) to raise it into. An agent will pick one, silently, ten times.

**PE-07 (S2) — Several FRDs reference change requests that do not exist as documents.** `LED-01` cites CCR-2 (registration of the `party_archived` error code), CCR-3 (the `POST /attachments` upload endpoint) and CCR-5 (the `ledger.notify_owner_on_staff_entry` setting key). `SAL-02` cites CR-SAL-2 (four settings: `sales.round_off_default`, `sales.require_hsn_b2b`, `sales.allow_free_text_lines`, `sales.auto_print`) and CR-SAL-3 (the `version` column and the idempotency store). Part 20 cites CR-BE-1 through CR-BE-5. Of these eleven, the upload endpoint is the most immediately blocking: `LED-01` FR-10 makes photo attachment part of the MVP's most-used flow and the endpoint it posts to exists only as a parenthetical reference to a change request that is not in the corpus. Part 20 §20.9 specifies the storage, validation and serving of attachments in detail and does not specify the create endpoint's request or response contract.

**PE-08 (S2) — The settings surface is large, scattered and has no schema.** From the features I read, the MVP requires at least: `ledger.credit_limit_mode`, `ledger.party_sms_on_entry`, `ledger.notify_owner_on_staff_entry`, `ledger.auto_sms`, `inventory.allow_negative_stock`, `sales.default_due_days`, `sales.round_off_default`, `sales.require_hsn_b2b`, `sales.allow_free_text_lines`, `sales.auto_print`, `sales.default_template`, numbering prefixes and padding per document kind, reminder templates, `fy_start_month`, `documents.terms`, locale and module toggles. The schemas themselves — the key list, each key's type, default, allowed values and which business-type preset seeds it — are not in the corpus. `PLT-06` is a never-cut MVP feature and it is a settings screen for a set of settings nobody has enumerated.

**PE-09 (S2) — The states-and-copy obligation converts every feature into a documentation task the FRD does not carry.** Canon §0.11 rule 6 forbids shipping without "loading/empty/error states, i18n keys in `en` and `hi`". Part 19 §19.11.3 makes a missing key a build failure. `LED-01` §8 specifies nine copy keys and their Hindi; the feature needs, conservatively, thirty — every field label, hint, placeholder, button, empty state, error per validation rule, error per business error code, snackbar variant and `aria-label`. Multiply by 74. Part 19 §19.11.7 says "a developer adds a key to `en.json` with the final English copy, and to `hi.json` with the Hindi copy from the FRD (every FRD §8 specifies both)" — which is true for the keys the FRD lists and false for the other two thirds, and the developer is then writing Hindi at 6 p.m. to unblock a build.

**PE-10 (S3) — The seed data is a normative requirement with no content.** §19.14.3 says "`seed_demo` is a normative requirement on the backend, not a nicety: the frontend's empty states, pagination, aging buckets, Hindi copy and print templates cannot be reviewed against an empty database, and E2E tests run against this seed." **PE-11 (S3) — `SAL-06` (draft autosave) has two storage tiers with an unspecified reconciliation.** Part 19 §19.5.7 gives a local snapshot every 500 ms to `localStorage` and a server draft after 3 s idle, on blur of party or date, and on `Ctrl+S`. ## 41.7 The Tech Lead

### 41.7.1 What is right

**The AI-coding-agent instructions are unusually good, and they are the right shape: prohibitions, not encouragement.** Part 20 §20.15 ("What an AI coding agent must not do") is twelve numbered rules, each of which maps to a real failure I have seen an agent produce: adding a dependency, putting logic in a serializer, using `float` for money, `UPDATE`ing a ledger row, reading `X-Tenant-Id`, returning 403 for a cross-tenant id, trusting client totals, skipping the audit row, writing a non-backwards-compatible migration, inventing an error code. Part 25 §25.19 and Part 26 §26.21 add per-side quick references. This is the single most useful artefact in the corpus for the way this product will actually be built.

**The code-review checklists exist per side (Part 25 §25.18, Part 26 §26.20) and the quality gates are mechanical.** Part 28 §28.11.2's nine merge conditions are all machine-checkable except the ninth, and the AST-based checks (tenant scoping, no-float, no-update-on-immutable, no `.objects.all()`) catch the four classes that review misses when it is tired. The rule that "`# noqa`, `eslint-disable`, `@ts-ignore` require a trailing `— reason: …`" is small and it works.

**The CI pipeline degrades gracefully to the solo phase.** §28.11.4 collapses the eight-stage pipeline to "two files and one runner" with the note that "the commands are the contract, the platform is not". That is the right design for a team that starts at one or two and becomes five.

**Flakiness has a policy with teeth** (§28.5.5): one retry in CI, zero locally; every retry recorded to a log file; three retries in fourteen days quarantines the spec for a maximum of two weeks; `waitForTimeout` banned; "flake is a defect in the product until proven otherwise". I would adopt this verbatim.

### 41.7.2 Findings

**TL-01 (S1) — There is no task breakdown, so there is nothing to assign.** Part 32 is referenced as the sprint plan by Part 13 §13.2 (which derives Phase 1's duration from it) and by Part 28 §28.6.2 (which fails the pipeline on unimplemented test IDs "for a feature marked done in **the task breakdown**"). It does not exist. The corpus has 74 features, each of which is between two days and six weeks of work, and nothing between a feature and a commit. On day one I cannot answer "what are the five of us doing this week" from these documents, and the traceability gate that is supposed to enforce done-ness has no input.

**TL-02 (S1) — The definition of done exists in four incompatible versions and the authoritative one is missing.** Canon §0.11 rule 6: FRD acceptance criteria, tests, permission checks, loading/empty/error states, i18n in both locales. Part 12 §12.8: product completeness, correctness, performance, security, operations, go-to-market — a launch checklist, explicitly "Part 35's per-feature equivalent" in reverse. Part 28 §28.6.3: every FR, BR, EC has a test; every AC green; permission matrix rows; isolation sweep; §9 states covered; `en`/`hi` keys; analytics events emitted and asserted. Part 25 §25.18 and Part 26 §26.20: review checklists. Part 35 is named as the owner and does not exist. §28.6.3's version is the most demanding and would, on `SAL-02`, require roughly seventy tests before the feature is done; nobody has agreed to that.

**TL-03 (S2) — The review load implied by the quality gates exceeds what a five-person team can sustain.** Per feature, a reviewer must verify: layering (Part 19 §19.1.2 and Part 20 §20.3.1), the ten-point `Ub*` checklist where components are touched, the invalidation-map row, the permission-matrix row, the isolation coverage, the query budget, the audit row, the idempotency support, the `en`/`hi` keys, the analytics events, the state coverage, the FRD's FR/BR/EC test mapping, and the migration's backwards compatibility. That is a thirteen-point review on a PR that may be four hundred lines. At a realistic four PRs per engineer per week and five engineers, that is twenty reviews a week at thirty to fifty minutes each — one to two full days of somebody's week, and in a five-person team it will be the same somebody. The mitigation is mostly available (the AST checks, the generated matrices, the i18n and bundle scripts already automate seven of the thirteen) but nothing in the corpus says which points are automated and which are human, so in practice all thirteen will be human until somebody builds the automation, which is PE-02's ten weeks.

**TL-04 (S2) — Onboarding a second developer is well served for structure and badly served for judgement.** Parts 19, 20, 25 and 26 tell a new engineer exactly where every file goes and what it may import; Part 19 §19.14.7 and Part 20's reference slice give a build order; `docker compose up` plus `seed_demo` gives a working environment. What is missing is the thing a new engineer actually needs in week one: a map of which decisions are settled and which are live, which chapters contradict each other and which side we took, and what the current open CRs are. The corpus is written as though it is internally consistent, so a new engineer's first discovery of a contradiction (there are at least ten, per PE-06) reads as their own misunderstanding, and they will resolve it silently the way a new person does.

**TL-05 (S2) — Technical-debt policy is absent, and two chapters create debt by design.** Part 19 §19.10.4 leaves explicit Phase 2 seams (the empty `offlineQueueSlice` at MVP, serialisable service payloads, idempotency keys as future queue ids); Part 20 §20.9.6 leaves `# storage-backend seam` comments for the S3 migration; Part 20 §20.10.4 pre-shapes the payment-aggregator adapter; Part 24 §24.1.2 requires that "Nothing in Shapes 1–2 may be built in a way that forecloses Shape 3". Every one of these is good practice. None of them has a register, an owner or a review date, so in eighteen months they are folklore. There is also no statement of what we do with debt we did *not* plan: no "fix it now / next sprint / never" rubric, no budget per sprint, and no definition of when a `TODO` is a defect.

**TL-06 (S2) — The branch and release process is not specified anywhere.** Part 28 §28.11.1 mentions a "merge queue" and "merge to `main`" and "release | tag"; Part 29 §29.6.1 requires "an **immutable tagged image**, never `latest` in production, so a rollback is a tag change" and §29.6.2 gives the release steps. Nothing states the branching model, whether releases cut from `main` or a branch, how a hotfix reaches production without carrying unreleased work, how the frontend and backend versions are kept compatible (they deploy independently per §20.12.2), what a release candidate is, or who may tag. Part 13 §13.6's release train has eight named releases at fixed week offsets and no mechanism.

**TL-07 (S2) — The velocity assumptions that are not stated are the ones that will break the plan.** Part 13 §13.2 assumes four to six engineers for 20–24 weeks. Unstated and load-bearing: that the FRDs are complete and correct (Part 18 C10 says the estimate "assume[s] the FRDs are complete before the build begins" — they are, but see PE-01); that `ml-uikit` and the BrandHub conventions are available and reusable (A10, with the consequence named as "several weeks" if false and no contingency); that the team has Django *and* Next.js *and* Indian GST domain knowledge, since the service layer is where the invariants live and the tax engine is where the compliance is; that nobody is also doing support, because Part 18 R9 requires "support ownership defined in writing with response targets" and the only engineers in the plan are the five building; that design is done ahead of engineering, since Part 23's components and the copy inventory (UX-07) are prerequisites, not parallel work; and that the pilot's ten merchants are recruited by somebody who is not in the 110 engineer-weeks.

**TL-08 (S2) — The test suite's speed budgets and its contents are inconsistent, and the budget is the thing that decides whether the suite is run.** §28.1.3 budgets the service-plus-selector band at under 90 seconds and the API band at under 180. §28.2 then mandates: a generated endpoint sweep issuing every read and write endpoint with a foreign id (roughly 120 endpoints); a list sweep asserting no foreign ids, `meta.total` against raw SQL, and the property preserved across `?ordering=`, `?q=` and every declared filter; a permission matrix of every endpoint × six principals (roughly 720 cells); money tests parametrised over "200 generated (payment, documents) combinations" and "500 generated documents" and "a 1,000-movement sequence"; and recompute tests that poison and rebuild. Each of those is a database round trip per case. Two of the three bands will exceed their budget by a wide margin, and §28.1.3's own warning applies: "a suite slower than this stops being run, and a suite that is not run is worse than no suite".

**TL-09 (S3) — The pre-push gate runs the service band and the pre-commit hook runs formatters, but nothing runs the AST checks locally.** §28.11.1 puts the AST checks (tenant scoping, no-float, no-update-on-immutable, no `.objects.all()`) in the PR stage only. Those are the four checks most likely to fire on a first draft and most annoying to discover after a push; they are also fast. Move them to pre-push.

**TL-10 (S3) — "One migration per PR" is a warning in one chapter and a rule in another.** Part 20 §20.12.1: "One migration per PR per app. If a PR needs three schema changes in one app, they are squashed into one migration before review." Part 28 §28.3.7: "One per PR | Advisory check: a PR touching more than one migration file prints a warning in CI." And §28.11.2 item 7: "at most one new migration per PR (warning)". A rule that prints a warning is a convention; state which it is.

**TL-11 (S3) — There is no on-call definition and Part 12 §12.8 requires one.** "Error budget, on-call route and a status page or its equivalent agreed" is a launch checklist line. Part 30 §30.10 describes alerting for "one operator, no pager", and Part 29's runbooks assume an operator. Who is that person, out of a team of five, and what happens on the second consecutive night?

### 41.7.3 Questions I would ask before committing to a date

1. What is the definition of done we are actually using, and does it include a test per BR and EC? That single answer moves the estimate by ten to fifteen percent.
2. Which of the thirteen review points are automated on day one, and which are human until PE-02's utilities exist?
3. What is the branching model, and how does a hotfix reach production?
4. Who reviews? In a team of five with this gate list, review is a role, not a rotation.
5. Are design and copy ahead of engineering by at least one module, and who owns that pipeline?
6. Who is on call, and who does support during the pilot?
7. Is the 22 weeks a commitment to a date or an estimate of a scope? I will plan very differently for each.

### 41.7.4 What I would add

- **A task breakdown at the level of a two-to-five-day slice**, generated from the FRDs rather than written: per feature, one slice per layer (schema+service, API+serializer, slice+thunk+service, screen, tests, copy), with the FRD's FR numbers attached to each slice. Roughly 350 slices for the MVP. This is Part 32 and it is the single missing artefact that blocks week one.
- **A `DECISIONS.md` at the repository root from the first commit**, holding the ten cross-chapter choices (PE-06) and everything after them, with date, choice, chapters affected and who decided. It becomes Part 39 when Part 39 exists. Two minutes per entry; it is the difference between a new engineer asking and guessing.
- **A "known contradictions" appendix in the onboarding doc**, updated as they are resolved. Telling a new engineer where the corpus disagrees with itself is the most respectful thing we can do for them.
- **A weekly ten-minute gate review** where the three automated gates that fired most are examined: if a gate fires constantly and is always overridden, it is wrong; if it never fires, it is not testing anything.
- **A debt register with three columns and a review date**: planned seams (the Phase 2 ones already named), unplanned debt, and expired debt (a seam whose phase arrived and which was not taken). Review at each phase boundary, which is where Part 13 §13.8 already puts every other decision.
- **A test-suite runtime budget enforced in CI**, failing the build when the service band exceeds 120 seconds, so that TL-08 is a build failure rather than a slow erosion. The fix is then a database strategy, as §28.1.3 says, rather than fewer tests.
- **A release checklist of ten lines**, not forty: tag, migrate, deploy, smoke, demo script, rollback rehearsed. Part 12 §12.8's forty-line checklist is the *launch* gate; a fortnightly release needs something a person will actually run.

### 41.7.5 Verdict

**Approve with conditions.** The engineering standards are the best part of this corpus and I would not change a line of Parts 25, 26 or Part 20 §20.15. What is missing is everything between the standard and the working week: there is no task breakdown, no agreed definition of done, no branching model, no debt policy, no on-call, and no channel for the ten known contradictions. Those are not documentation gaps; they are the mechanism by which a five-person team turns 110 engineer-weeks into a product, and without them the first two sprints will be spent inventing them under pressure. Conditions: TL-01 (a task breakdown before sprint one), TL-02 (one definition of done, chosen and costed), TL-06 (branch and release process written), and TL-03 answered by naming which gates are automated on day one.

## 41.8 The CTO

### 41.8.1 What is right

**The local-first deployment is a commercial weapon and it has been treated as one.** ADR-019's four-service compose stack, ADR-012's cron-driven scheduler with no broker, ADR-013's local disk, ADR-014's client-side PDF and ADR-021's short allow-list add up to a product that a bank's information-security team can review in a week rather than a quarter. Part 11 §11.2 states the reason correctly: "A product that runs on a single VPS with PostgreSQL and no managed services is sellable into places a Kubernetes-plus-Redis-plus-S3 product cannot enter without a six-month security review." Neha Raghavan's persona (Part 14 §14.7) is written from the procurement side and it is accurate — she will forward the dependency list to a reviewer, and a short list is the product feature.

**The security posture is genuinely good for this stage.** Tenant isolation with five layers of defence and a generative test sweep; 404 rather than 403 for cross-tenant ids, tested; httpOnly cookies with CSRF double-submit; refresh-family reuse detection that revokes the family; a permissions-version claim that makes a role downgrade take effect in seconds without a revocation list; impersonation requiring recorded consent, time-boxed to five minutes, read-only, audited per request rather than per session, and announced to the merchant. Part 27 §27.9's DPDP mapping is the most complete I have seen from a company this size, and §27.9.3's honest statement that a deletion request colliding with a 72-month GST retention is "honoured by redaction of personal fields with retention of financial rows, and the merchant is told exactly that" is the right answer legally and commercially.

**The partner isolation invariant is the thing that makes the channel sellable.** Part 24 §24.1.3's last row — the partner "cannot read the shop's book", ever, except through consented impersonation — is what allows a bank to resell this without its own merchants treating it as surveillance, and §24.1.4's five rules ("no partner code in code", one database, one bundle, resolution not configuration, defaults all the way down) keep the fan-out at zero. That is a genuinely hard thing to hold and it is held.

### 41.8.2 Findings

**CTO-01 (S1) — Client-side PDF is the decision that will be most expensive to reverse, and the reversal is already scheduled.** ADR-014 makes the browser's print path the entire document story and defers server-side PDF to Phase 2 "only because automated sending needs a file". Count what needs a file: `LED-07`'s automated reminders if they are ever to carry a statement; `NTF-05`/`LED-12`'s WhatsApp utility templates, which take document attachments; `NTF-06` email; `RPT-14`'s scheduled reports; `PLT-10`'s export bundle; `SAL-14`'s customer-facing invoice page; and any partner who wants the merchant's invoice emailed under the partner's domain (`WLB-06`). So Phase 2 acquires a second rendering engine — WeasyPrint — that must produce a document visually identical to the React print component, for four templates, in two paper sizes, with Devanagari, with tenant branding and with a QR code. Two renderers for the same legally-significant document is a permanent correctness and support liability: the merchant prints one thing and their customer receives another, and the difference is discovered by the customer. The cheaper path is to decide now that the server is the renderer for documents that leave the product, and that the browser print path renders *the same HTML* served by the server, rather than a parallel React component tree.

**CTO-02 (S1) — The minimal-dependency stance's total cost of ownership has been counted on one side only.** ADR-021 is justified by the security review and the migration cost of every dependency. The cost on the other side is the eighteen first-party implementations enumerated in PE-02: two QR encoders, a service worker, a job queue with lease and dead-letter semantics, an idempotency store, a feature-flag system, an analytics pipeline, a traceability joiner, three performance harnesses, two image compressors, a contrast checker, a bundle checker, an i18n checker and extractor, an HSL ramp generator, a structured-logging stack and an APM middleware. Each is code Metis writes once and maintains, security-reviews and ports forever, and each is code a partner's reviewer will also read — a hand-written service worker and a hand-written QR encoder are *more* alarming to a reviewer than Workbox and a well-known QR library, not less. My position is that the stance is right for the *runtime* dependencies that a reviewer counts (no Redis, no S3, no broker, no third-party SDK in the request path) and wrong for build-time and test-time tooling, where it buys nothing and costs weeks. The ADR does not make that distinction and it should.

**CTO-03 (S1) — No Celery is correct at 500 tenants and has no stated failure boundary.** ADR-012's `platform_job` design is good engineering. The concerns are three. First, the reliability of the thing the collection journey depends on: `LED-07`'s D-1/D0 reminders fire from a daily job, deduplicated by `scheduled_key` per date, and a day on which the job was never enqueued leaves no row, no dead letter and therefore no alert (BE-03, and Part 30 §30.10 alerts on dead letters and CRITICAL only). Silent non-delivery of the collection nudge is the failure mode that loses the merchant, and it is currently undetectable. Second, the scaling shape: the periodic jobs fan out inside a single handler — §20.8.7 says "`sales.refresh_overdue` runs once with `tenant_id = NULL`, iterates active tenants" — which is O(tenants) inside one execution at a 5,000-tenant target, holding Part 29's global advisory lock (BE-04). Third, there is no stated threshold at which the runner is replaced. §20.8.1 says "if Celery is ever adopted, `enqueue()` changes and no caller does", which is the right seam; what is missing is the number that triggers it.

**CTO-04 (S2) — Build-versus-buy, surface by surface.** My assessment where the corpus has made a choice:

| Surface | Decision | My view |
|---|---|---|
| Database | PostgreSQL 16 in Docker, dev and prod | Right, unreservedly |
| Queue | Own, `platform_job` | Right at this scale; needs a replacement trigger (CTO-03) |
| Cache | None; denormalised tables | Right, and the reasoning ("durable, shared, transactional and recomputable, which a memory cache is not") is better than most teams manage |
| Object storage | Local disk, settings-switch to S3 | Right; the seam is real and the two conditional call sites are named |
| PDF | Client-side | **Wrong** (CTO-01) |
| SMS / WhatsApp | Adapters, console at MVP | Right; the adapter shapes are already written |
| Payments | Manual UTR at MVP, aggregator Phase 2 | Right, and honest about what the product cannot see |
| Error reporting | None at MVP, Sentry settings-gated Phase 2 | **Marginal.** `logClientError` is a one-function seam, but NFR-14/15 commit to crash-free-session and uncaught-error rates that cannot be measured without a sink. We are gating on a number we cannot compute |
| Feature flags | Own | Right; it is three tables and a resolver |
| Analytics | Own batcher + own sink endpoint | **Marginal.** Building the sink means building retention, querying and dashboards too; Part 31 §31.8 assumes dashboards exist |
| QR | Own, twice | Wrong twice over (FE-09) |
| Service worker | Own | Marginal; Workbox is build-time, not runtime |
| Test/build tooling (traceability, perf, contrast, bundle, i18n) | Own | Wrong. These are build-time; the allow-list should not reach them |
| Uptime monitoring | External free tier | Right |
| Backups | `pg_dump` + rsync, verified nightly, restore drill monthly | Right, and the "an unverified backup is a hypothesis" framing should be quoted to every startup |

**CTO-05 (S2) — The Content-Security-Policy is deferred to Phase 2 and the threat it addresses is the one used to justify the auth design.** Part 19 §19.7.1 chooses httpOnly cookies specifically because "one `dangerouslySetInnerHTML` in a print template — and print templates are exactly where teams reach for raw HTML — would exfiltrate a readable token". Part 19 §19.14.2 then says "A Content-Security-Policy is added at the reverse proxy in Phase 2 rather than in `next.config.js`, so that the same header set covers the API and the media endpoint too." The reasoning for the location is sound; the deferral is not, because the product renders user-entered party names, item descriptions, notes and terms on public share links served to arbitrary recipients. A CSP with `default-src 'self'` and no `unsafe-inline` is a nginx block in the production overlay, available on day one.

**CTO-06 (S2) — NFR-37 requires a vulnerability scan on every build; the pipeline runs one nightly.** "Vulnerability scan on every build with no high or critical findings at release" (Part 18 §18.8.4) versus Part 28 §28.11.1, which places "dependency audit (`pip list --outdated`, `npm audit`)" in the nightly job only, and §28.11.2's nine merge conditions, which do not include it. `pip list --outdated` is not a vulnerability scan; it is a freshness check. With a short allow-list the exposure is small, but the commitment is to a control that is not implemented.

**CTO-07 (S2) — The scaling path is credible for one axis and silent on the other two.** Part 29 §29.10 and Part 20's notes give the vertical path (bigger VPS, read replicas via a database router with `selectors/` as the routing seam, Kubernetes at Phase 3 when a partner needs it). What is not addressed: the *multi-partner* path, where one partner requires its own deployment for data residency (OQ-05 and OQ-06 both flag this and both are due "before first partner contract"), which turns one deployment into N deployments with N upgrade windows, N backup regimes and N restore drills — and Part 24 §24.1.4 rule 2 ("one database, one schema… the scaling path never addresses partners") explicitly declines to solve it. And the operational path: at 5,000 tenants the single operator model of Part 30 §30.10 (a daily email digest, immediate emails for dead letters, one external uptime check) is the same model as at 50. The trigger for hiring an operator is not named.

**CTO-08 (S2) — Knowledge and operational concentration is the risk nobody owns.** One deployment, one database, one operator persona (Arjun Menon, Part 14 §14.8), a monthly restore drill performed by that person, a super-admin console (`PLT-14`) that is first on the cut list, and a team of four to six engineers with no operations hire in the plan. Part 12 §12.8 requires "on-call route agreed" and Part 29 §29.11's runbooks assume a reader. If the person who wrote the deploy runbook is on a plane, the corpus is the runbook — which is an argument for the runbooks being as good as they are, and not an argument that the bus factor is acceptable. Note also that the restore drill, the migration rehearsal on a production copy (NFR-19) and the PITR upgrade (§29.5.4) are all "when the first partner goes live", which is the busiest week the company will have.

**CTO-09 (S2) — Vendor and platform risk is well isolated technically and unowned commercially.** Every external surface is behind an adapter with a null implementation and Part 18 §18.11's dependency rule ("No MVP flow may block on a third-party surface") is enforced. What is missing is ownership and lead time: DLT registration is "Compliance owner" with a 2–4 week lead, and no compliance owner is named anywhere in the corpus despite being one of four required signatures on Part 18 §18.16; the SMS provider is OQ-03, due "before launch", unanswered, and it determines whether registration is per-Metis or per-partner, which changes `WLB-06`'s design and the template registry's scoping; the payment aggregator is OQ-04, due Phase 2 entry, and determines who holds merchant KYC; the GSP is Phase 3 with no shortlist. UPI is the lowest-risk of these because nothing calls out. The single highest-risk one is DLT, because without it the feature Part 11 §11.1 calls the category's most-praised behaviour is inert at launch (PM-08).

**CTO-10 (S3) — `X-Accel-Redirect` and the storage seam are specified; the media path's tenant check is the one place a byte can leak.** §20.9.4 keeps the authorisation decision in Django and hands bytes to nginx, which is correct. Part 29 §29.7's media configuration is where this can silently regress to a public `MEDIA_URL`, and the deployment-shape test (§28.3.9) checks compose service names and entrypoints but not that `/media/` is unroutable. One test.

**CTO-11 (S3) — The alerting channel is SMTP and SMTP is not a configured dependency at MVP.** Part 30 §30.10 routes immediate alerts and the daily digest through "`platform.alert_operator` job → SMTP (the one outbound the MVP allows, to a fixed operator address)". Part 18 §18.11's dependency table has no email surface; `NTF-06` (email channel) is Phase 2; Part 29's env catalogue sets `EMAIL_BACKEND: console`. The operator's only detection mechanism therefore prints to a log by default.

### 41.8.3 The two or three decisions most expensive to reverse

1. **Client-side PDF (ADR-014).** Reversing it in Phase 2 means two renderers for one legal document, or re-templating everything. Decide now.
2. **Redux without a server-state library (ADR-004).** Not because Redux is wrong, but because the hand-written invalidation map (FE-02) will be threaded through seventy-odd features; introducing a query library afterwards means rewriting every hook and every slice. This is a one-way door that closes in about week six.
3. **Application-only tenant isolation with RLS deferred (ADR-008).** Adding RLS later means auditing every query path and every management command for the session variable, on a live database, under a partner's security review. Adding it now costs a migration and a connection-level `SET`.

Behind those three, the decision I would most want re-examined is the *scope* of the dependency allow-list (CTO-02), because it is the one whose cost is being paid invisibly, in weeks that Part 13 does not contain.

### 41.8.4 Questions I would ask before funding this build

1. Who is the compliance owner? Four signatures are required and one of them has no name.
2. Is DLT registration started? If not, start it this week; it is the longest lead item and it gates the product's trust story.
3. Is the partner deployment model one Metis-operated instance or one per partner (OQ-05)? That answer changes the operational plan, the upgrade process and the hiring plan, and it is currently due "before first partner contract", i.e. after the architecture is built.
4. What is the trigger to adopt a queue, a cache, an error-reporting sink and an operations hire? Four numbers.
5. Does the allow-list apply to build and test tooling? If it does, we are paying eight to twelve weeks for it; if it does not, say so in ADR-021.
6. What do we do about a second renderer for PDFs — server-render now, or accept two?

### 41.8.5 What I would add

- **An ADR-021 amendment distinguishing runtime from tooling dependencies.** Runtime stays closed and short, because that is the list a partner's reviewer reads. Tooling opens to a named, versioned, small set (Workbox, a QR library, a coverage plugin, a load tool) with the same ADR discipline but a lower bar. This recovers most of PE-02's ten weeks without weakening the commercial argument at all.
- **Server-rendered documents from day one**, with the browser print path rendering the same server HTML. One renderer, one template, one source of truth for what the merchant's customer sees. WeasyPrint is a single Python dependency and it is the one exception I would spend the ADR on.
- **A replacement-trigger table** as an appendix to Part 0: for each deliberately simple choice (no queue, no cache, no object store, no error sink, no RLS, one operator, one deployment), the metric and the threshold at which it is revisited, and who watches the metric. This is the artefact that turns "we will do it later" into a plan.
- **A CSP in the production nginx overlay at MVP**, plus a test that `/media/` is not directly routable.
- **A per-partner deployment decision recorded before `WLB-03` is designed**, because it is the difference between one upgrade a fortnight and N.
- **A named second operator and a quarterly bus-factor drill**: somebody other than the author of the runbook performs the restore, the deploy and the partner provisioning, from the runbook, once a quarter. If they cannot, the runbook is wrong.
- **A vulnerability scan in the PR pipeline**, not nightly, with the allow-list making it cheap.

### 41.8.6 Verdict

**Approve with conditions.** The architecture is coherent, the security posture is above market for this stage, and the local-first deployment is a real commercial asset that the team has correctly identified and protected. My conditions are about the decisions that are cheap today and expensive in eight months:

1. CTO-01: server-side document rendering decided now, one way or the other, with the consequence for Phase 2 written down.
2. CTO-02: ADR-021 amended to separate runtime from tooling, and the recovered weeks returned to the plan.
3. CTO-03 and CTO-07: replacement triggers named for the queue, the cache, error reporting, RLS and the operations hire.
4. CTO-05 and CTO-06: CSP at MVP and the dependency scan moved into the PR pipeline.
5. CTO-09: a compliance owner named and DLT registration started before the build, not before the launch.

I would fund the build on those conditions. I would not fund it with the PDF decision left open, because that one compounds.

## 41.9 The CEO

### 41.9.1 What is right

**The market thesis is correct and it is the only one in this category I would back.** Part 18 §18.2 states it in four paragraphs: the Indian small business keeps four records and reconciles them by hand; the software market has split into a free ledger cluster that could not be paid for and a paid billing cluster that spends two rupees to earn one; the merchant who needs both runs two apps. The evidence is real and it is cited — a hundred crore of revenue against a hundred and sixteen crore of loss at the category leader; a competitor reaching profitability only by abandoning lending and charging thirty to ninety-nine rupees a month; roughly two percent conversion on ten million registrations at the paid incumbent. The conclusion — that the gap is not a feature but an architecture, with the udhaar ledger as the spine and everything else hanging off it — is the right conclusion, and Part 11 §11.2's defensibility argument for it is the strongest thing in the commercial case.

**The refusals are as valuable as the plan.** No lending in the core flows, no advertising with no reversal trigger, no double-entry accounting, no storefront, no payroll. Each with the evidence that produced it. In this category the companies that failed did so by adding, and a founder who has written down fourteen things he will not build — with the conditions under which he would change his mind — has done the hardest part of strategy before the first line of code.

**The partner channel is identified as the route to market rather than the app store,** which matches how software actually reaches Indian small businesses: Tally with twenty-eight thousand partners, Vyapar with twenty-seven thousand. And Part 24's design treats the partner as an architecture rather than a logo upload, with the one invariant — the partner cannot read the merchant's book — that makes a bank able to resell it without the merchant feeling surveilled.

**The pilot's exit criterion is a real question asked of real people.** "At least five of ten merchants report at day 45 that they no longer maintain the paper khata." That is the only criterion in this document that cannot be gamed, and making it the phase's actual exit rather than a feature count is the decision of someone who wants to know the truth.

### 41.9.2 Findings

**CEO-01 (S1) — There is no financial plan.** Forty-one chapters specify the product to the paisa and nowhere state what it costs to build, what the runway is, or what is being raised. From the plan's own numbers: four to six engineers for 20–24 weeks in Phase 1, 16–20 in Phase 2, 20–24 in Phase 3 — roughly fourteen to seventeen months of a five-to-seven person team before the twelve-month target of ₹35–45 lakh ARR. At Indian loaded engineering cost that is on the order of ₹1.5–2.5 crore of spend against ₹0.35–0.45 crore of revenue at month twelve, before design, compliance, support, hosting, DLT, field onboarding and the founder. The 24-month goal of "spend-per-rupee-of-revenue below 1.5" against Vyapar's 2.04 is a good target and it is unreachable at month twelve on these numbers; the plan implies it is reached later, and does not say when. The only lever the corpus contains for responding to a funding constraint is Part 12 §12.7's cut list, which cuts *features* — it does not cut phases, people or markets, which is what a runway constraint actually cuts.

**CEO-02 (S1) — Phase 2 is contingent on a partner signature and there is no alternative plan.** Part 13 §13.3's entry criteria require "a named partner with a signed intent", the phase's exit is "one partner live on its own hostname… with at least ten of its merchants transacting", and thirteen of its thirty-seven features serve the partner. Part 18 §18.14 lists partner dependency as a risk and mitigates it by not building speculatively — which controls the waste and leaves the calendar exposed. If no partner signs by the end of Phase 1, a quarter of the roadmap has no contents and the revenue line that Part 1 §1.9 calls "the second revenue line" does not begin. That is the largest single-point risk in the company and it is owned by nobody, because signing a partner is not in the 110 engineer-weeks and there is no business-development plan in the corpus.

**CEO-02a (S2, corollary).** The partner archetypes in Part 24 §24.1.1 are banks, NBFCs, fintechs, FMCG distributors, ERP resellers and CA networks. The sales cycle for the first two is six to twelve months through procurement, security review and legal, and Part 13 §13.3 asks for the signature at the *start* of Phase 2, which begins around week 24. To have it by then, the conversation starts in week zero, in parallel with the build, and the person having it is the founder. That is a plan; it is simply not written down anywhere.

**CEO-03 (S1) — We have two incompatible answers to how we make money, and the difference is our whole differentiation story.** Part 1 §1.9 puts the paid wall at "multiple devices and users with roles, inventory and GST billing depth, server-sent messaging"; Part 11 §11.2 lists "staff roles without a desktop licence" as a differentiator competitors cannot copy because it is a pricing-model conflict for them. If staff roles are free, our differentiation is real and the paid wall loses its strongest lever, so the wall must be stock depth plus server-sent messaging plus exports — which is a narrower, later-arriving wall and it moves the revenue curve right. If staff roles are paid, we are selling the same thing our competitors sell at a lower price, which is a promotion and not a position. This is a decision only I can make and the document has made it twice, in opposite directions, and then built `PLT-15` to enforce whichever one we pick without telling anyone what the limits are (PM-02).

**CEO-04 (S2) — The unit economics are stated as ratios and never as a per-merchant cash flow.** The corpus gives: ₹12,000–15,000 blended revenue per paying tenant; ≥ 75 % gross margin at 24 months; CAC payback under 12 months direct and under 6 months partner; per-active-merchant pricing for partners with an unstated price. It does not give: the cost of the twenty-minute field onboarding that Part 18 A2 makes the adoption assumption, at a field-agent day rate; the support cost per merchant per month, when Part 1 §1.10 names support as the second of five risks and the category's lowest sub-score; the hosting cost per tenant on a single VPS at 500 and at 5,000; or the per-message cost once DLT and a provider exist, on a feature (`LED-08`) that fires on every ledger entry for every opted-in party — which for Ramesh Agarwal's 140 customers at a few entries a day is a five-figure annual SMS bill against a ₹12,000 subscription. That last one is not a rounding error; it is a potential negative gross margin on the product's most-praised feature, and nothing in the corpus meters it.

**CEO-05 (S2) — The conversion arithmetic in our own success table does not reconcile** (see PM-06). At 12 months the table asks for 300 paying out of 1,500 monthly active with 5 % self-serve conversion and ≥ 30 % of revenue from partners, which implies roughly 210 self-serve paying tenants — 14 % of active, not 5 %. At 24 months it asks for 2,000–2,500 paying out of 10,000 active. I am not saying the targets are wrong; I am saying that three numbers in one table imply three different conversion rates and I cannot tell which one I am being asked to believe. If the real answer is that the partner channel carries conversion, then the partner channel is not the second revenue line — it is the business — and CEO-02 becomes existential rather than important.

**CEO-06 (S2) — The competitive window is nine to eleven months and no competitor response is modelled.** Phase 1 is 20–24 weeks and Phase 2 is 16–20 more before the channel is operable. In that time the plausible responses are: a ledger incumbent with fifty million installs ships a GST invoice; a billing incumbent ships a free khata tier; a payments platform bundles a free ledger with its QR product to defend its merchant relationship. The corpus analyses competitors in depth (Parts 4, 9) and models responses nowhere. Part 11 §11.2 asserts that a ledger app adding invoicing "gets two balances" — true, and it does not need to be elegant, it needs to be adequate, and it will arrive faster than our Phase 2. What survives each response, and which of our eleven differentiators are actually load-bearing, is the analysis I would want before committing eighteen months.

**CEO-07 (S2) — The regulatory exposure is well handled in the product and unowned in the company.** GST rate history with effective dates, thresholds as configuration, DPDP mapped obligation by obligation with a retention schedule, DLT template discipline, a breach runbook with the Data Protection Board's timelines and CERT-In's separate requirement, an under-18 block, a grievance contact. That is a better compliance posture than most Indian SaaS companies have at Series A. And Part 18 §18.16 requires a compliance owner's signature on the PRD, Part 13 §13.4 requires "a compliance owner named — an individual accountable for the correctness of IRN payloads, e-way bill validity windows and the interest computation's tax treatment", OQ-03 assigns the DLT question to "Compliance owner", and no such person exists. The product's compliance is designed; the company's compliance accountability is a placeholder.

**CEO-08 (S2) — We are shipping without the feature the research says makes the category work, and then measuring whether the category works.** `LED-08` — the SMS the customer receives when something is written against their name — is called "the single most-praised behaviour in the category… it makes disputes disappear" (Part 11 §11.1). It is config-gated and inert until DLT registration and a provider exist, and Part 12 §12.8 explicitly permits launching with the channel "provably inert". The pilot then asks ten merchants at day 45 whether they have stopped keeping paper. If fewer than five say yes, we will not know whether the product is wrong or the channel was off, and that is the number the whole of Phase 1 exits on.

**CEO-09 (S2) — What could kill the company, in order, and what the plan does about each.**

The company half — who the agents are, what they cost, who employs them — is entirely unaddressed, and without a partner there are no agents |

**CEO-10 (S3) — The e-invoicing threshold is a churn trigger we can see coming and do not warn about.** Part 18 A5 states that a tenant crossing ₹5 crore "cannot legally use the product for B2B invoices until `SAL-12` ships in Phase 3, and this must be stated in sales conversations rather than discovered". `SAL-12` lands at launch + 34 weeks. `RPT-07` already computes the tenant's outward supplies. A merchant who crosses the threshold and discovers it from their CA rather than from us is a merchant who leaves and tells the CA why — and the CA has sixty clients.

**CEO-11 (S3) — English and Hindi is right for launch and the corpus knows the third language question is open and unowned in a way that matters commercially.** OQ-08 asks "Third language: which, and at what phase?", owned by Product, due at Phase 2 exit. The personas already include a Gujarati-speaking distributor in Surat and a Gujarati-speaking manufacturer in Rajkot (Part 14 §14.2, §14.4), and the partner archetypes include FMCG distributors whose networks are regional by construction. The i18n architecture is ready; translation and support capacity are not. A partner in Gujarat or Tamil Nadu will ask for their language as a condition, and the answer will need to be a price and a timeline rather than "Phase 2 exit".

### 41.9.3 The single most important thing to get right

The eight-second entry, posted to a ledger whose balance is never wrong, for a merchant whose eighty opening balances somebody typed in on day one. Everything else in these forty-one chapters is recoverable by a patch release. Those three things — speed, correctness and migration — are the product, and the third of them is the one we have specified and not staffed.

### 41.9.4 Is the plan ambitious enough, or too ambitious?

Both, on different axes, and that is the problem.

**Too ambitious on breadth.** Seventy-four features across twelve modules, four to six engineers, twenty-two weeks, a closed dependency list that turns eighteen library choices into eighteen build items, four print templates verified on physical printers, thirty-eight design-system components, a thirty-five-spec end-to-end suite and a forty-line launch checklist. The Product Engineer's bottom-up estimate is 122 engineer-weeks against 110 available and that is before the ten cross-chapter contradictions are resolved. We will ship late or we will ship the cut list, and the cut list starts by removing the console our only operator depends on.

**Not ambitious enough on the thing that compounds.** The partner channel is the whole commercial thesis and it is entirely in Phase 2, gated on a signature, with no owner and no plan. Every week of Phase 1 in which no partner conversation happens is a week subtracted from a twelve-month sales cycle. The right shape is to run the partner conversation from week zero with a Phase 1 product that is deliberately demo-able to a partner — which it already is, because Part 12 §12.6's eight-minute demo script is a partner demo — and to let the first partner's requirements shape Phase 2 rather than waiting for Phase 2 to create the partner.

### 41.9.5 Questions I need answered before I commit the company to this

1. What does this cost, what is the runway, and what are we raising? Nobody has written a number.
2. Is multi-user free or paid?
3. Who owns partner business development, starting this week, and what is the target date for the first signed intent?
4. If no partner signs by week 24, what is Phase 2?
5. Who is the compliance owner, and has DLT registration started?
6. What is the cost per merchant of the twenty-minute onboarding, and who pays it?
7. What is our SMS cost per active merchant per month once `LED-08` is live, and does it fit inside ₹12,000 a year?

### 41.9.6 What I would add

- **A one-page operating plan** alongside this specification: headcount by month, spend by month, runway, the three milestones that unlock the next tranche, and the two numbers we would stop on. Forty-one chapters of product and zero pages of company is a real imbalance.
- **A named partner pipeline with five logos and a first-conversation date**, started in week one of the build, with the eight-minute demo (Part 12 §12.6) as the asset. The demo already exists; it is being treated as a smoke test and it is also our entire sales collateral.
- **A partner-independent Phase 2** adopted as the default (the Product Manager asks for the same thing for different reasons), so that the roadmap does not have a hole in it if a signature slips.
- **A per-merchant contribution-margin model** with four inputs — onboarding cost, support cost, hosting cost, messaging cost — reviewed at each phase boundary against actuals. `LED-08`'s per-message cost is the input most likely to surprise us.
- **A turnover-threshold warning in-product** (`RPT-07` already has the data), plus a stated policy for what a tenant does when they cross it before `SAL-12` ships.
- **A pricing page, published, before launch**, because Part 12 §12.8 already makes it a gate and because a published price with a renewal commitment is itself a differentiator in a category where renewal price rises are the third-ranked churn cause.
- **A support plan for the first hundred merchants** that does not consist of five engineers, with a cost and an owner, because the research says this is where products in this category die.

### 41.9.7 Verdict

**Approve with conditions — and my conditions are not about the product.** The specification is extraordinary and I would not change the thesis, the scope discipline or the refusals. What I will not commit the company to is a plan that has no financial dimension, no owner for the partner channel that carries the commercial case, and two contradictory answers to how we charge. Conditions, in the order I need them:

1. CEO-03: the paid wall decided this week, and every chapter amended to match.
2. CEO-01: an operating plan with headcount, spend, runway and stop conditions.
3. CEO-02: a named owner for partner business development with a week-one start, and a partner-independent Phase 2 written as the default.
4. CEO-07: a compliance owner appointed and DLT registration started before the build.
5. CEO-04: a per-merchant contribution model, with the `LED-08` messaging cost computed before we turn the channel on.

With those five, I would commit. Without the first three, we would be building the right product on a plan that cannot tell us when to stop.
