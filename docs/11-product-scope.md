# Part 11 — Product Scope

Scope is the most expensive decision in this product and the easiest one to lose. The category DigiKhaato enters contains two proven shapes — the free ledger app that reached fifty million installs and could not be paid for, and the paid billing-and-inventory app that earns four thousand rupees a year and takes two rupees of spend to earn one rupee of revenue. Both shapes are known. What is not known is whether the thing in between can be built without becoming either a toy or a small ERP, and the answer depends almost entirely on what is left out.

This part fixes the four lists an engineer or product owner needs in order to answer "should we build this?" without reopening the strategy each time. **Common** is what the category has and DigiKhaato must therefore have. **Differentiating** is what DigiKhaato has and the category does not. **Avoid** is what DigiKhaato will not build, with the condition that would have to be true to reverse the decision. **Simplify** is what the category over-builds and DigiKhaato ships in a deliberately reduced form. Every item names the feature IDs that carry it, so the lists are checkable against Part 16 rather than being opinions.

The closing section, §11.6, is the decision procedure: a short, ordered set of tests to apply to a request that arrives mid-build. It exists so that a scope question can be answered in ten minutes by the person holding it, instead of waiting a week for a meeting.

## 11.1 Common — the table stakes

An item belongs on this list when a merchant evaluating DigiKhaato against Khatabook, OkCredit, Vyapar or myBillBook would notice its absence within the first session and treat that absence as disqualifying. Table stakes are not differentiators and must not be built as if they were: they must be correct, fast and unremarkable.

**The two-button party ledger with a running balance.** `LED-01`, `PTY-03` and `LED-04`. A red "You gave" and a green "You got", an amount, an optional note, a date defaulting to today with backdating allowed, and a balance that updates instantly. Every competitor in the ledger cluster has exactly this, the colour convention is stable across all of them, and inverting or complicating it would be read as a bug. The research is unambiguous that speed here is the product: the top-ranked complaint against the market leader in 2026 is that the app "hangs while entering transactions". Parity is not enough; this interaction has a measured budget (`LED-01` §1: entry saved in eight seconds, drawer open in a hundred milliseconds) because slowness in this one flow invalidates everything else.

**Opening balances and a way in from paper.** `LED-02`, `PTY-10`, `INV-09`, `IMP-01`. Day-one adoption is entirely a migration problem: the merchant already has thirty to three hundred parties with balances written in a notebook. Apps that make the merchant re-key that by hand lose them in week one. DigiKhaato supports typing an opening balance on the party form, importing parties with balances from CSV, and importing items with opening stock, all through the same validate-preview-commit framework.

**Customer-side transparency.** `LED-08`, `LED-04`, `NTF-03`, and `PTY-09` in Phase 2. The single most-praised behaviour in the category is that the customer receives a message when something is written against their name, because it makes disputes disappear. A ledger only one party can see is a notebook with extra steps. DigiKhaato ships the transaction SMS as a configuration-gated MVP feature (`M*`), the shareable statement with a running balance as a core one, and the WhatsApp deep-link share that costs nothing and needs no approval.

**Reminders that the merchant does not have to compose.** `LED-05`, `LED-06`, `LED-07`. Asking for money is socially awkward; a templated message is neutral, and that neutrality is the feature. Parity requires three things together: a per-party collection date, one-tap manual reminders over WhatsApp, SMS and phone, and automated nudges the day before and on the due date. The market leader's D-1/D0 default is a good default — it collects without nagging — and DigiKhaato adopts it rather than inventing a cadence.

**A GST-compliant tax invoice, and the non-GST alternatives.** `SAL-02`, `SAL-01`, `SAL-03`. Compliance is the reason Indian small businesses buy software at all. The invoice must satisfy CGST Rule 46 in full — consecutive series of at most sixteen characters unique per financial year, HSN or SAC, quantity with a UQC, taxable value after discount, CGST/SGST or IGST shown separately by slab, place of supply for inter-state supplies, reverse-charge flag — and must equally produce a Bill of Supply for composition dealers and an Estimate for the kachha-bill habit that precedes it. A product that can only produce one of these three does not fit the market it is aimed at.

**Sharing and printing that match how merchants actually work.** `SAL-03`, `PAY-04`, `NTF-03`. A4 and 80 mm thermal templates, WhatsApp share, a public link. The evidence that WhatsApp sharing is used is that its breakage is a top-ranked complaint, not a feature request.

**An item master with stock, and low-stock visibility.** `INV-01`, `INV-02`, `INV-03`, `INV-07`, `INV-08`. Name, SKU, barcode, category, unit, HSN, tax rate, purchase and selling price, MRP, reorder point, on-hand. Stock that moves when a bill is made and a purchase is recorded, a movement history with document links, and a dashboard bucket for items below their reorder point. Every billing-cluster competitor has this; without it the product cannot claim to replace the stock register.

**Purchases that feed stock and the supplier ledger together.** `PUR-01`, `PUR-02`, `PUR-03`. A purchase bill posts goods in at cost, updates weighted-average cost, and credits the supplier's ledger in one transaction. Merchants track what they owe suppliers in the same notebook as what customers owe them; separating the two into unrelated screens is how the multi-app trap begins.

**Payments with the modes people actually use, and a UPI QR.** `PAY-01`, `PAY-02`, `PAY-03`. Cash, UPI, bank, cheque, card, other; a reference or UTR; allocation to open documents; a split receipt across modes — this last one appears in the research specifically as a complaint that a leading competitor does not track it accurately. The static UPI QR generated from the tenant's own VPA costs nothing to provide and is what a merchant expects to see on an invoice in 2026.

**Expenses and a cashbook.** `EXP-01`, `EXP-02`, `EXP-03`. Not accounting — a day-wise view of cash and bank in and out with opening and closing figures, which is the only "accounting" most owner-operators do.

**The standard report set.** `RPT-01` through `RPT-08`. Dashboard, day book, sales and purchase registers, receivables and payables aging, stock summary, GST summary as a GSTR-1/3B preparation aid, and CSV or Excel export from all of them. The accountant is a second, non-paying user in almost every small business; the product that does not hand data cleanly to them is returned.

**Multi-business, multi-user and roles.** `PLT-04`, `PLT-05`. Owners routinely run two businesses from one phone number; staff must be able to enter without seeing everything. The four system roles of canon §0.9 — owner, admin, staff, accountant — are the parity shape, and putting them behind a desktop licence as the incumbents do is itself the differentiator (§11.2).

**Mobile-number OTP sign-up, and Hindi.** `PLT-01`, `PLT-02`, plus the `en`/`hi` requirement that runs through every FRD. Merchants will not fill forms; identity is the phone number they already give customers. Language is not decoration: the owner-operator in this market is typically between forty-five and fifty-four years old, and more than half of small businesses report that finding and setting up digital tools is hard.

**Credit notes and voids.** `SAL-04`, `SAL-05`, `PUR-04`, `PAY-05`. Returns happen, bills get cancelled, payments get recorded twice. A product that cannot undo an action without deleting a row will be abandoned the first time a mistake is made in front of a customer.

**Data portability and deletion.** `PLT-10`, `IMP-02`. Under DPDP this is an obligation; commercially it is a trust signal. A merchant who can export everything is a merchant who is not afraid to start.

## 11.2 Differentiating — and why each is defensible

An item belongs here when it is absent from, or badly served by, every product in the competitive set, and when copying it would cost a competitor something real — an architectural change, a business-model conflict, or a habit they cannot break.

**One book instead of four apps.** The category is split: ledger-first products are free, multilingual and phone-only but weak at GST and stock; billing-first products are paid, English-heavy, desktop-led and treat udhaar as "receivables" in an accounts-receivable screen. A merchant who wants both runs two apps and reconciles by hand. DigiKhaato makes the udhaar ledger the spine and hangs billing, stock, purchases, payments and expenses off it, with `LED-10` guaranteeing that every document posts its own ledger entry with a link back to its source. **Defensibility:** this is an architectural commitment, not a feature. A ledger app that adds invoicing bolts a documents table onto a transactions table and gets two balances; a billing app that adds udhaar gets a receivables report with a Hindi label. Retrofitting the spine means rewriting the core of either product.

**Business-type tuning that only changes defaults.** `PLT-03` asks for the business type at onboarding and uses it to seed defaults — inventory on or off, document kinds, units, party labels — and canon is explicit that it "never hard-wires behaviour". A service business does not see stock fields; a wholesaler sees credit limits and aging first. **Defensibility:** competitors either ship one dense interface for everyone (and collect the complaint that it "overwhelms less tech-savvy owners") or fork into vertical SKUs that multiply their support cost. Seeding defaults from one codebase is cheap to maintain and impossible to copy without the same discipline about where behaviour may branch.

**Immutability and correction-as-reversal as a user-visible promise.** `LED-03`, `PLT-08`, plus canon's first non-negotiable rule. Ledger entries and stock movements are never updated or deleted; a correction posts a reversal and a replacement linked by `supersedes_id`, with a mandatory reason, and the statement offers a toggle to show or hide corrections. The audit log records who did what, when, with before and after. **Defensibility:** the second-most-common complaint across the whole category is data integrity and sync — balances that change, entries that vanish after an update, "cost me thousands" reviews. Competitors that shipped mutable, soft-deletable transaction rows cannot retrofit immutability without a migration that rewrites every historical balance. This is also the single feature that makes the product's data underwriting-grade, which is what a lending partner actually wants to buy.

**Credit control treated as a business function, not a consumer nicety.** `PTY-06` (limits with `off`/`warn`/`block` modes and an audited override), `LED-05` (collection dates and buckets), `LED-09` (aging in 0–30/31–60/61–90/90+), `RPT-05`. Mobile apps in this market expose due dates but not limits; the incumbents expose limits but only on desktop tiers costing five to twenty times more. **Defensibility:** this is where udhaar stops being a kirana feature and becomes a wholesale one, and the wholesale and distribution segment is where the money and the multi-user need both live. Copying it requires the ledger spine (above) plus a permission model that can distinguish "may post an entry" from "may override a limit".

**Staff roles without a desktop licence.** `PLT-05`, and the permission codenames of canon §0.9 enforced on every endpoint. In the competitive set, multi-user is the paid desktop hook: one product sells multi-device login as its mid tier, another gates roles to a plan costing thousands of rupees a year, and the incumbents sell LAN users. **Defensibility:** it is a pricing-model conflict for them, not a technical one. A competitor whose paid conversion is driven by the multi-user gate cannot open it without losing the revenue line that justifies its valuation.

**Honest payment reconciliation.** `PAY-01` with its manual UTR path at MVP, `PAY-07` (unmatched payments queue) and `PAY-06` (aggregator webhooks) in Phase 2. The number-one collection complaint in the category is "money debited, ledger not updated", and it happens because a static QR carries no party context and nothing calls the merchant's system back. DigiKhaato's answer is layered and states its own limits: dynamic intent links carrying a reference that maps to a party, a visible queue for credits that arrived without context with one-tap mapping and a learned payer-VPA-to-party association, and — at MVP, before any aggregator exists — an explicit "I received it by UPI, here is the UTR" entry that is one field, not a workaround. **Defensibility:** competitors with payment-aggregator revenue have an incentive to hide the unmatched case rather than surface it; a queue that says "three payments arrived that we could not place" is an admission they are reluctant to make.

**Vernacular parity across the whole product, not just the ledger half.** Every FRD requires `en` and `hi` copy keys for every string, including the invoice form, the stock adjustment reason list and the GST summary. The ledger-cluster competitors ship ten to thirteen languages in the ledger and English in the billing tier; the billing-cluster competitors collect "missing multilanguage support" as a standing complaint. **Defensibility:** this is a discipline cost paid continuously, from the first commit. It is trivially copyable in principle and very rarely copied in practice, because retrofitting i18n into a mature billing UI is a months-long project with no visible feature at the end.

**White-label as an architecture, not a logo upload.** `WLB-01` through `WLB-06`, `PLT-14`, `PLT-15`. A Partner record owns branding defaults, allowed modules, entitlements, support contact and legal footer; tenants inherit and may override within Metis-granted limits; branding is applied at runtime through CSS variables and document templates rather than by building a second app. **Defensibility:** the market evidence is that every distribution owner — payments platforms, banks, telecoms, FMCG brands — has built or bought some merchant tool and stopped at the layer adjacent to its own revenue. None has built ledger plus billing plus inventory, and none is known to license one. The channel, not the app store, is how software reaches Indian small businesses: the incumbents move volume through tens of thousands of partners. A product designed for partner resale from the first migration is a different product from one that adds a branding screen in year three.

**GST that knows its own history.** The `tax_rate` table carries effective dates, because the slab structure changed on 22 September 2025 and an invoice dated before that must still compute with the old rates when it is reprinted, amended or credited. **Defensibility:** low technical difficulty, high consequence, and an area where products that hard-coded a rate enum quietly produce wrong numbers on historical documents.

**No ads, no lending banners, and a price that does not move.** This is a product decision with teeth: the top complaint against the second-largest ledger app is intrusive advertising, the top negative theme for the largest is loan-processing delays polluting a bookkeeping app, and renewal price hikes rank third across the whole category. DigiKhaato's paid boundary is multi-device, staff, stock depth and server-sent messaging — never the entry itself. **Defensibility:** it is a business-model choice a competitor funded on a lending thesis cannot copy.

**Deployable by the partner, on one machine.** ADR-019's docker-compose of four services, ADR-012's cron-driven scheduler with no Celery or Redis, ADR-013's local disk storage, ADR-014's client-side PDF, and ADR-021's short dependency allow-list. **Defensibility:** partners in this market include banks and NBFCs with data-residency requirements and procurement departments that count third-party dependencies. A product that runs on a single VPS with PostgreSQL and no managed services is sellable into places a Kubernetes-plus-Redis-plus-S3 product cannot enter without a six-month security review.

## 11.3 Avoid — what DigiKhaato will not build

Each item states what is excluded, why, and the trigger that would have to be met to reverse the decision. The triggers are deliberately hard. An item may only leave this list through a Class C change under Part 10 §10.7, with the trigger demonstrably satisfied and the decision recorded in Part 39.

## 11.4 Simplify — capabilities shipped deliberately small

These are not omissions. Each is a capability the category over-builds, shipped in a reduced form that covers the behaviour the target user actually performs, with a stated growth path so the reduction does not become a dead end.

**Warehouse management reduced to locations.** The category ships bins, zones, pick lists, pack lists, put-away rules and cycle-count schedules. DigiKhaato ships one default location at MVP (`inventory_location` exists with a single row so nothing needs migrating later), and `INV-11` adds a locations master and a transfer document that posts one out-movement and one in-movement atomically in Phase 2. Nothing else. A merchant with a shop and a godown needs to know how much is in each and to move stock between them; the operations a warehouse-management system exists to optimise do not occur in a thirty-square-metre godown.

**Price lists reduced to a per-party default.** At MVP an item has a selling price and a line may override it; that is the whole pricing model, and it matches how a counter sale is actually priced. `INV-13` in Phase 2 adds named lists — typically "retail" and "wholesale" — and a per-party default so the line price defaults correctly for a distributor's customers. What is not built: quantity break tables, date-effective price schedules, customer-group matrices, margin-floor rules, currency variants. The wholesale case is one list per customer, not a pricing engine.

**Approval workflows reduced to reason capture.** No product in this segment has a working approval chain, because the person who would approve is standing next to the person who acts. DigiKhaato replaces the workflow with accountability: every destructive or financial state change — void, reverse, correct, archive, write-off, credit-limit override — requires a typed reason of at least three characters through `UbReasonDialog`, shows the consequences in plain language before it is confirmed ("Stock +2, Ledger −₹898"), writes an audit row with before and after, and is visible in `PLT-08`. Permission separates who may do it at all: staff post entries, owners and admins correct them. This delivers the outcome an approval chain is bought for — nothing significant happens unattributed — at a fraction of the interface.

**Costing reduced to weighted average.** One method, applied everywhere: purchase bills update `avg_cost`, stock valuation is on-hand times average cost, and the Phase 2 profit summary (`RPT-10`) computes cost of goods sold from it. No FIFO, no LIFO, no standard costing with variance analysis, no lot-level cost. Weighted average is what the segment's accountants expect for general trade, it is the only method that survives backdated entries without a rebuild, and it is the method `PUR-08` (landed cost) can extend by adjusting the same average.

**Tax reduced to GST with rate history.** Slabs from the `tax_rate` table with effective dates, intra-state versus inter-state resolved from place of supply, composition and unregistered handled by document kind, reverse charge as a flag. No tax groups, no jurisdiction matrices, no tax-authority-specific reporting beyond the GSTR-1/3B preparation aid. A tax engine general enough for two countries is a tax engine nobody can verify against either.

**Reports reduced to a fixed set with good filters.** Eight MVP reports (`RPT-01`–`RPT-08`), each with a date range, the filters that report needs, a totals row that reflects the filtered set, and CSV or Excel export. No report builder, no saved custom reports, no pivot designer, no scheduled delivery until `RPT-14` in Phase 3. The research is clear that what merchants and their accountants actually do with a report is export it, so the export is the extension point.

**Document numbering reduced to a series per kind per financial year.** `platform_document_sequence` holds tenant × kind × FY with a prefix and a next number, resetting on 1 April, constrained to the sixteen-character alphanumeric-with-slash-and-hyphen rule of Rule 46. No numbering expression language, no per-location or per-user series, no gap-filling. The compliance requirement is that the series be consecutive and predictable; anything more configurable makes it easier to produce a non-compliant series.

**Custom fields reduced to notes and tags.** A party has notes and free tags (`PTY-05`) used for areas, routes and categories; an item has a description and a category; a document has notes and terms. There is no field designer. Tags carry the grouping needs the research surfaced — area-wise collection, route-wise lists — without a schema that must be validated, indexed, exported and localised per tenant.

**Roles reduced to four.** Owner, admin, staff, accountant, defined once in canon §0.9 with a fixed permission matrix, enforced identically in the API and the UI. `PLT-12` (custom roles built from permission codenames) is Phase 3 and exists because the codenames are already the primitive; until then the answer to "our cashier should not see reports" is the staff role, which already cannot.

**PDF reduced to two client-side templates.** A4 and 80 mm thermal, rendered as React print components with tenant branding and printed through the browser (ADR-014), with the public share link rendering the same view. No template designer, no server-side rendering farm, no per-tenant layout editing. Server-side PDF arrives in Phase 2 only because automated sending needs a file, not because the print output is inadequate.

**Notifications reduced to an inbox plus adapters.** `NTF-01` gives one in-app inbox with unread counts and a fixed set of types; `NTF-02` gives an adapter interface with a console backend and a template registry. There is no per-user notification preference matrix, no digest scheduler and no channel routing rules at MVP. The per-party opt-outs that DPDP and TRAI actually require exist; the preference surface that enterprise products ship does not.

**Search reduced to per-list server search.** Every list has a debounced search over the fields that list is about, with server-side filtering and pagination. There is no global omni-search across modules at MVP. The navigation is shallow enough that the merchant knows which list they are in.

## 11.5 How the four lists interact

The lists are not equally weighted and are not applied in isolation.

A **Common** item that is slow, ugly or unreliable is worse than a missing **Differentiating** item, because parity failures are what get noticed in the first session and differentiators are what get noticed in the second month. When the two compete for the same week of engineering, parity wins.

A **Differentiating** item that depends on a **Simplify** decision must not quietly un-simplify it. Credit control is a differentiator; it is delivered by a nullable limit, three modes and an audited override — not by a credit-approval workflow. If delivering a differentiator requires reversing a simplification, that is a Class C change and the simplification's growth path should be examined first.

An **Avoid** item that keeps arriving is information, not noise. The trigger conditions in §11.3 exist to be checked periodically — at each phase boundary, against real telemetry and churn interviews — not to end the conversation permanently. What they prevent is the reversal happening implicitly, one small feature at a time, which is how a ledger app becomes an unfinished accounting package.

## 11.6 Scope-boundary rules for a request that arrives mid-build

Apply these tests in order to any request — from a customer, a partner, a stakeholder or an engineer's own good idea — and stop at the first one that answers.

1. **Does it already have a feature ID?** Look it up in Part 16. If it exists with phase `2`, `3` or `F`, the answer is "yes, in that phase", and the conversation is about whether the phase should change (Class C), not about whether to build it. Most requests end here.
2. **Is it an item on the Avoid list?** If yes, the answer is no, and the reply should name the trigger from §11.3 rather than the refusal. A trigger tells the requester what evidence would change the answer, which is a more useful answer than "out of scope".
3. **Is it the un-simplified version of something on the Simplify list?** If yes, the answer is the simplified version plus the growth path. "We need approval for large discounts" is answered by reason capture, a permission and an audit row — and if that genuinely does not solve it, the request should be re-stated as the outcome, not the mechanism.
4. **Does it make an existing Common item correct, faster or more reliable?** If yes, it is not a new feature; it is a defect or a performance fix against an existing feature ID, and it goes into that feature's backlog at the priority its budget in §5 of the FRD implies.
5. **Does it serve a persona in Part 14 who is already a target, in a journey in Part 15 that already exists?** If it serves a persona the product does not target, or invents a journey nobody described, it is a new market, not a new feature.
6. **Can it be expressed as a change to defaults rather than behaviour?** Business type, tenant settings (`PLT-06`), module toggles and entitlements (`PLT-15`) are the designed answer to "we work differently". A surprising proportion of requests are satisfied by a setting that already exists or a default that is wrong for a business type.
7. **What does it cost across all seven surfaces?** Schema, API, service layer, UI, permissions, i18n in both languages, tests. Canon's sixth engineering rule means a feature is not shipped until all seven exist. A request that looks like two days of UI is rarely two days.
8. **What does it displace?** In a fixed build, a new item is an exchange. If the requester cannot name what moves out of the phase to make room, the request is not yet a proposal. If they can, it becomes a Class C change with an impact statement and a decision-log entry.

If a request survives all eight tests, it is a legitimate scope change: raise it under Part 10 §10.7, add the catalogue row, write the FRD section, and record the decision. If it survives seven and fails the eighth, hold it in the open questions register of Part 18 §18.15 for the next phase boundary. Nothing is lost by being written down and deferred; a great deal is lost by being built between two other things without a specification.
